**Key figures**: Vicente García de la Huerta (1734–1787, playwright), King Alfonso VIII of Castile (1155–1214, historical subject), Raquel (historical figure, 12th century), Leandro Fernández de Moratín (later neoclassical heir)

## Summary

*Raquel*, a tragedy in five acts by Vicente García de la Huerta, premiered in Madrid on October 30, 1778, at a moment when Spanish dramatic literature was undergoing sustained reform toward neoclassical principles. The play dramatizes one of medieval Castile's most persistent legends: the love affair between King Alfonso VIII of Castile (r. 1158–1214) and Raquel (*La Fermosa*), a beautiful Jewish woman of Toledo, whose influence over the king—and her eventual death at the hands of outraged nobles—had been a subject of Spanish historical chronicle and literary treatment since the thirteenth century.

García de la Huerta's version unfolds in the classical idiom: five acts in verse, strict observance of the three unities (single action, single setting, time compressed to approximately one day), and a moral architecture focused on the collision between private passion and public responsibility. The king's infatuation with Raquel leads him to neglect his duties and isolate himself from the court and clergy; the nobility's intervention, culminating in Raquel's death, restores order at the cost of the king's happiness. The work draws on an earlier treatment by Lope de Vega (*Las paces de los reyes y judía de Toledo*, c. 1610–15), but García de la Huerta recasts Lope's baroque proliferations into the austerity that neoclassical theory demanded.

The verse is written in Spanish *décimas*—ten-line stanzas with the rhyme scheme *abbaaccddc*—a demanding form that García de la Huerta handles with formal precision. The play was drafted during a particularly difficult period: García de la Huerta spent twelve years in exile in Oran, Algeria (1765–1777), a banishment tied to political conflicts at the Spanish court, and composed significant portions of *Raquel* during this period. Its 1778 premiere, following his return from exile, marked the play's public debut on a major Madrid stage and its introduction to the wider European theatrical world.

## The Playwright: Vicente García de la Huerta

García de la Huerta was born in 1734 in Zafra, Extremadura, and educated at the University of Salamanca, where he developed interests in classical literature and Spanish Golden Age drama. He held the position of court librarian at the Biblioteca Real in Madrid and established himself as both a poet and literary critic. His *Theatro Hespañol* (1785), a sixteen-volume anthology of Spanish drama, became an influential—and polemical—statement of his critical principles, emphasizing the value of native Spanish dramatic tradition over French neoclassical imports.

This ambivalence ran through *Raquel* itself. García de la Huerta embraced neoclassical form but rejected the argument of some Spanish reformers that Spanish dramatic tradition should simply be displaced by French models. His play demonstrated that neoclassical rigor and Spanish material could coexist—that the formal demands of Corneille's tragic world could be applied to subjects drawn from Castilian medieval history. This position put him in conflict with Spanish neoclassicists who saw older Spanish drama as a barbaric obstacle to Enlightenment reform.

His return from Oran exile in 1777 coincided with a moment of relative opportunity: King Charles III's reformist administration was actively cultivating Spanish cultural life, including the theater, as part of a broader program of Enlightenment modernization. The premiere of *Raquel* in 1778 thus represented both a personal vindication and a cultural intervention.

## The Historical Legend: Alfonso VIII and Raquel

The legend underlying *Raquel* derives from accounts in the *Primera Crónica General* (compiled under Alfonso X, c. 1270–84) and later chronicles. Alfonso VIII, whose reign saw the disaster of the Battle of Alarcos (1195) and the triumph of Las Navas de Tolosa (1212), was said to have fallen deeply in love with a Jewish woman of Toledo known as *La Fermosa* ("The Beautiful One"). Medieval accounts alleged that the king spent seven years—a number likely symbolic—neglecting his realm and his queen, Eleanor of England (daughter of Henry II), before outraged Castilian nobles killed Raquel to restore the king to his duties.

Historians have not established Raquel's historical reality with certainty; the legend may blend an actual liaison with literary and legendary elaboration. But the story became a touchstone for Spanish meditation on kingship, passion, religious tension (Christian king and Jewish woman), and the obligations of rulers to their people—themes that retained resonance across centuries. García de la Huerta's decision to dramatize this legend in 1778 was not politically innocent: the question of how monarchs balanced personal will against the demands of their kingdoms was very much alive in Bourbon Spain.

## The Neoclassical Context

*Raquel*'s appearance in 1778 placed it alongside other major neoclassical dramatic events in Europe. Earlier in the same year, [Voltaire's final tragedy *Irène*]({{ site.baseurl }}/news/arts/irene-voltaire/) had premiered at the Comédie-Française in Paris in March 1778—the aged Voltaire, too ill to attend, following the audience's reception by dispatch—the supreme demonstration of French neoclassical tragedy's prestige. In Italy, the operatic world was convulsed by the [Gluck-Piccinni controversy]({{ site.baseurl }}/news/arts/gluck-piccinni-querelle-1778/) over the proper form of opera seria, and [La Scala had opened]({{ site.baseurl }}/news/arts/la-scala/) in Milan that August. The cultural landscape of 1778 was defined by contests over the proper relationship between tradition and Enlightenment reform—and *Raquel* was Spain's contribution to that argument.

Unlike the Gluck-Piccinni debate, which played out in the cosmopolitan terms of pan-European musical controversy, *Raquel*'s contest was distinctly national. The question it implicitly posed was whether Spanish drama could participate in the European neoclassical achievement on its own terms—drawing on native historical material and resisting total assimilation to French models—rather than simply imitating French tragedy.

## Reception and Controversy

*Raquel*'s reception in 1778 was substantial and not without controversy. Contemporary critics and audiences recognized the play as a major achievement in Spanish serious drama, and it quickly ran to multiple performances in Madrid. Subsequent historians would confirm its status as the finest tragic work of eighteenth-century Spain. But the play was also caught in the polemical crossfire between reformist neoclassicists and defenders of the Spanish theatrical tradition.

García de la Huerta's later anthology *Theatro Hespañol* (1785) excluded most neoclassical plays in favor of the Spanish Golden Age canon—a choice that drew attacks from younger reformers such as Leandro Fernández de Moratín, who saw it as reactionary. The irony was considerable: García de la Huerta himself had authored the century's greatest neoclassical tragedy, yet refused to align fully with the neoclassical party in literary politics. His position—neoclassical in form, nationalist in cultural argument—made him a paradoxical figure in Spanish literary history.

## The Broader 1778 Context

The October 1778 premiere of *Raquel* in Madrid unfolded against a backdrop of significant political developments in Spain. King Charles III was contemplating entry into the American Revolutionary War; Spain would formally join the conflict in 1779 through the Treaty of Aranjuez, and diplomatic pressure was mounting throughout 1778 under the leadership of [the Conde de Floridablanca]({{ site.baseurl }}/news/people/floridablanca/), Spain's chief minister. The success of *Raquel* at this moment represented a cultural assertion: Spanish drama, Spanish letters, and Spanish intellectual life could match the achievements of France and Italy. The play's themes of statecraft—a king's private weakness threatening the stability of his realm—also carried contemporary resonance in an era when questions of monarchical authority and institutional responsibility were alive across the Atlantic world.

García de la Huerta would live only nine more years after *Raquel*'s premiere, dying in 1787. He spent much of that period in literary controversy, completing his anthology and defending his critical positions. His reputation endured principally on the strength of *Raquel*, which remained the reference point for Spanish neoclassical drama well into the nineteenth century.

## Significance

*Raquel* represents the apex of Spanish neoclassical tragedy and a watershed in the consolidation of that dramatic form within Spanish letters. Its 1778 premiere demonstrated that Spanish dramatists could absorb the formal and philosophical vocabulary of neoclassicism and deploy it with emotional and intellectual force. The play's success vindicated Spanish cultural achievement at a moment when Spanish Enlightenment thinkers were anxious about their nation's standing relative to France and Italy.

Several dimensions of its significance extend beyond Spanish literary history:

**European context**: *Raquel* established that neoclassical tragedy could be written in a national idiom drawing on non-classical medieval history—that the form was not necessarily a vehicle for ancient Greek and Roman subjects. This broader application of neoclassical principles to native historical material would become increasingly common in European drama by the end of the century.

**The Enlightenment's aesthetic politics**: The controversy over *Raquel*'s place in the Spanish tradition illustrated the contested politics of cultural reform: modernizers and nationalists drew the same evidence in opposite directions, using the same play to argue both for neoclassical adoption and for the defense of native tradition.

**Legacy in Spanish letters**: *Raquel*'s influence persisted through the romantic period, when Spanish historical drama flourished. The subject itself—Alfonso VIII and the Jewess of Toledo—attracted later treatments, including the German poet Franz Grillparzer's *Die Jüdin von Toledo* (1872), demonstrating that García de la Huerta's dramatization had injected the legend into European literary consciousness.
