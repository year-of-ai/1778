## Summary

Catherine II, known as Catherine the Great (1729–1796), was the Russian Empress whose diplomatic mastery and strategic vision shaped European great-power politics in 1778. By the time she ascended the Russian throne in 1762, at the age of 33, Catherine had transformed herself from German-born Sophie Friederike Auguste into one of Europe's most formidable political actors. In 1778, at age 49, she wielded unprecedented influence over European affairs through carefully calibrated diplomacy: she offered to mediate the War of the Bavarian Succession between Austria and Prussia, threatened military intervention to shape its outcome, and ultimately orchestrated a negotiated settlement that prevented a wider continental war while Russia remained engaged in the Ottoman conflicts. Her foreign minister, Nikita Panin, executed much of this strategy through the "Northern System," a diplomatic network designed to establish Russian predominance in Northern Europe and the Baltic. Catherine's 1778 diplomacy was calculated to preserve Russian strategic interests while appearing to serve Europe's broader peace — a balancing act that defined her entire 34-year reign.

## Strategic Context and the 1778 European Crisis

Catherine's 1778 position of influence rested on three decades of Russian military expansion and institutional innovation. The Seven Years' War (1756–1763) had already demonstrated Russian capacity to intervene decisively in Central European affairs; Catherine's subsequent wars against the Ottoman Empire (1768–1774 and 1787–1792) and the partitions of Poland (1772, 1793, 1795) had steadily extended Russian territory westward. By 1778, when the War of the Bavarian Succession erupted on July 3 following the death of the Bavarian elector Max Joseph, Catherine faced a diplomatic choice: whether to allow Prussia under Frederick the Great to gain territorial advantage at Austria's expense, or to intervene to maintain the European balance of power — and thereby preserve Russia's leverage over both Austria and Prussia.

The Bavarian Succession crisis was precipitated by Frederick the Great's invasion of Bohemia on July 5, 1778. Frederick sought to claim Bavaria for himself or at minimum prevent Joseph II of Austria from inheriting the entire Bavarian state. Maria Theresa, the Austrian co-ruler and widow of Francis Stephen, initially supported her son Joseph's ambitious claims, but the military stalemate that developed through late 1778 (what would be called the "Potato War" for its supply constraints) proved economically devastating. Neither side could achieve decisive victory; both faced mounting costs; yet neither was willing to yield without external pressure. This deadlock created the exact opening Catherine's diplomacy required.

Russia's military weight in 1778 was considerable: the army numbered approximately 300,000 regulars and could mobilize additional frontier forces; the navy had been rebuilt following its destruction in the Seven Years' War and had demonstrated its effectiveness in the Battle of Chesme (1770), destroying the Ottoman fleet in the Aegean. Catherine's offer of 50,000 troops in support of her mediation was therefore credible — neither Frederick nor Joseph could dismiss it as an idle threat. The offer was simultaneously generous (appearing to offer real assistance to whichever power Catherine chose to support) and calculated (the 50,000 troops were a small fraction of Russian strength, suggesting Catherine could escalate if needed).

## Catherine's Mediation Strategy

Catherine's approach to the Bavarian Succession reflected her calculation that direct Russian military involvement in Western Europe would be ruinously expensive and strategically dispersive. According to her diplomatic correspondence and the records of her foreign ministry, Catherine's stated position was that she "wanted to mop up the spoils of war for the Russian Empire but did not want to get involved in another costly European conflict." This apparent contradiction — seeking advantage without cost — shaped her strategy. She offered to mediate the dispute, acting as an impartial arbiter; simultaneously, she signaled to both Austria and Prussia that Russia was willing to commit fifty thousand troops to augment whichever side Russia chose to support. This dual offer — mediation with the implicit threat of military intervention — gave Catherine negotiating leverage over both powers.

Her mediation efforts proved particularly effective against Joseph II. When Joseph discovered that his mother Maria Theresa was secretly negotiating for peace behind his back, he "furiously offered to abdicate," according to contemporary accounts. Catherine's threat of military support combined with the knowledge that even his own co-ruler was seeking an exit from the war convinced Joseph to reconsider his aggressive posture. By mid-September 1778, Joseph had withdrawn troops from Bohemia and signaled willingness to negotiate. Catherine's foreign minister, Nikita Panin, coordinated these negotiations through diplomatic channels, with France's Comte de Vergennes working in parallel to prevent the Bavarian crisis from expanding into a full European war that would have forced France to abandon its support for the American Revolution.

## The Teschen Settlement and its Aftermath

Catherine's mediation facilitated negotiations that culminated in the Treaty of Teschen (signed May 13, 1779 but substantially negotiated through Catherine's network in 1778). The treaty established a settlement with three main territorial provisions: Austria retained the Innviertel (a strip of Bavarian territory it had occupied in early 1778, comprising approximately 2,000 square kilometers with a population of roughly 100,000); Prussia gained confirmation of its rights to the Franconian Hohenzollern territories of Ansbach and Bayreuth upon the extinction of their ruling lines; and the succession to Bavaria itself was confirmed for Charles Theodore of the Palatinate and then the House of Zweibrücken. This formula gave both Austria and Prussia face-saving claims while preventing either from achieving their original maximalist objectives.

More importantly for Catherine, the treaty was guaranteed by both Russia and France, a diplomatic formula that acknowledged Russian prestige and locked Russia into the settlement as a guarantor power. This arrangement gave Catherine a permanent seat at the table of European great-power diplomacy without requiring the enormous military expenditure that a war in Central Europe would have entailed. The explicit Russian role as treaty guarantor — the first time Russia had been formally named as a guarantor of a Central European peace settlement — represented a significant elevation of Russian diplomatic status, equivalent in symbolic terms to the great-power recognition Britain, France, and Austria had long enjoyed.

Catherine's success in the Bavarian Succession crisis demonstrated her understanding of a principle that would define her entire reign: that Russian power was best exercised through diplomatic leverage rather than frontal military commitment. She had shaped the outcome to Russia's advantage (preventing Prussian aggrandizement), maintained good relations with Austria and France (both desirable allies), and preserved Russian resources for her own imperial expansion (the Ottoman wars and the Polish partitions). Her willingness to offer military support without actually deploying it; her ability to coordinate with Vergennes across vast distances without formal treaties; and her strategic reading of the domestic political crisis between Joseph and Maria Theresa — all demonstrated diplomatic skill of the highest order.

## The Broader Context of 1778: Catherine and the American Revolution

Catherine's 1778 diplomacy cannot be separated from her position on the American Revolutionary War, which was reshaping European alignments. Russia officially maintained neutrality in the Anglo-American conflict, yet Catherine was deeply interested in its outcome. An independent American republic would further weaken British naval hegemony — a goal Catherine shared with France and Spain, for whom British dominance represented a permanent threat. However, Catherine was unwilling to formally commit Russian resources to the American cause; the transatlantic conflict seemed too distant from Russia's immediate sphere of interest (the Ottoman Empire, Poland, and Central Europe) to warrant direct involvement.

This calculation led Catherine to a sophisticated position: she tacitly supported French intervention in America through diplomatic recognition and open hostility to British interests, while reserving Russian military resources for the conflicts that directly affected Russian territory and influence. The Bavarian Succession crisis of 1778 represented an opportunity to demonstrate Russian diplomatic weight in the very European theater where Catherine was most active, while leaving Anglo-American affairs to France and Spain. Her mediation thus served a dual purpose: it resolved a crisis that might have forced Russia to choose sides in a European war, and it established Russian prestige as indispensable to any future European settlement.

## Catherine, Potemkin, and the Domestic Architecture of 1778 Diplomacy

The year 1778 coincided with a pivotal moment in Catherine's domestic political life: her relationship with Grigory Potemkin — her closest advisor, probable secret husband, and the dominant figure in Russian strategic policy — had entered its most productive phase. Potemkin, appointed Governor-General of New Russia and Azov in 1776, was in 1778 actively organizing the administrative and military apparatus that would enable the annexation of Crimea in 1783. While Catherine managed the Bavarian Succession crisis from St. Petersburg, Potemkin was building the infrastructure of Russian expansion in the south: founding the city of Kherson on the Dnieper in 1778 (construction began in June 1778), organizing the Black Sea Fleet, and negotiating with Crimean Tatar factions to prepare the ground for annexation.

The parallel operations — Catherine's diplomatic intervention in Central Europe and Potemkin's military construction in the south — were not incidental. They reflected Catherine's conscious strategy of managing simultaneous theaters of engagement: the western (Bavarian Succession) and the southern (Ottoman/Crimean). By demonstrating diplomatic mastery in the west, Catherine strengthened her hand against the Ottomans in the south; by threatening military intervention in Bavaria, she signaled to the Porte that Russia possessed reserves of force beyond those already committed to the Black Sea frontier. Potemkin's Kherson construction gave Russia a naval base capable of projecting power into the Mediterranean — the same theater where the War of the Bavarian Succession, if it had escalated to a general European war, might have drawn in Ottoman forces. Catherine's 1778 diplomacy was thus not merely European; it was simultaneously an Ottoman-facing strategic maneuver.

## The Panin System and Russian Diplomacy

Catherine's 1778 mediation role was orchestrated through the diplomatic network established by Nikita Panin, her foreign minister from 1763 to 1781. Panin had conceived of the "Northern System" — a network of alliances among Russia, Prussia, Denmark, Sweden, and the German principalities designed to exclude France and Austria from dominance in Northern and Central Europe. The Bavarian Succession crisis tested and ultimately modified this system: Catherine chose to coordinate with France (through Vergennes) to resolve the crisis rather than allow it to shatter the European balance entirely. This pragmatic abandonment of Panin's ideological preference for a Russo-Prussian axis in favor of Russo-French cooperation demonstrated Catherine's willingness to adapt diplomatic strategy to immediate circumstances.

By 1778, Panin was aging (he would be replaced by Ivan Osterman in 1781), and Catherine was consolidating direct control over foreign policy. Her decision to mediate the Bavarian Succession was her own, not Panin's, and it set a precedent for the more active Russian role in European diplomacy that would characterize the 1780s and 1790s.

## Significance

Catherine the Great's 1778 mediation of the Bavarian Succession was a masterwork of diplomatic brinkmanship and strategic calculation. She prevented a wider European war that would have forced France to abandon America, maintained Russian prestige without excessive expenditure, and positioned Russia as the indispensable arbiter of European peace. Her willingness to threaten military intervention while actually committing Russia only to diplomatic guarantees of the settlement exemplified the kind of strategic thinking that made Russia a great power.

Catherine's diplomatic success in 1778 emboldened her for the more ambitious projects of the 1780s and 1790s: the wars against the Ottoman Empire that expanded Russia to the Black Sea, the successive partitions of Poland that made Russia a major European land power, and the Russo-Prussian antagonism that would define Central European politics for the next century. The Bavarian Succession crisis was, in a sense, her dress rehearsal for these larger dramas. Her success against Joseph II and Frederick the Great — achieved without committing Russian armies and without breaking with either power — demonstrated that in the age of enlightened absolutism, diplomatic skill was as valuable as military force.
