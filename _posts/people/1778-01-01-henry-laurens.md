## Summary

Henry Laurens (1724–1792) was a wealthy South Carolina merchant and political leader who reached the pinnacle of his influence in 1778 when he served as President of the Continental Congress, the office he had assumed on November 1, 1777 following the resignation of John Hancock. Unlike some earlier congressional leaders, Laurens brought a merchant's precision and legal-commercial sensibility to an often-chaotic deliberative body. His South Carolina wealth derived from rice, indigo, and slave trading — a commerce he had participated in for decades, though by 1778 he had become increasingly ambivalent about slavery and would eventually free his enslaved workers in his will. In Congress, Laurens handled the critical threshold events of 1778 with formal rigor: the rejection of the Carlisle Peace Commission's overtures; the ongoing state-by-state ratification of the Articles of Confederation; and the confirmation of the Franco-American alliance treaties negotiated by Benjamin Franklin, Silas Deane, and John Adams in Paris and signed on February 6, 1778.

## Merchant Career and Rise to Congressional Leadership

Laurens was born in Charleston (then Charles Town), South Carolina on March 6, 1724, into a prosperous Huguenot family. He built his commercial fortune as a factor and merchant — trading in deerskins, rice, and indigo, and for a period operating as one of the largest slave traders in colonial North America, importing and selling thousands of enslaved Africans through the Charleston slave market during the 1750s and 1760s. By the late 1760s he had become deeply conflicted about the trade, publicly declining new consignments and eventually withdrawing from it entirely. His commercial network extended to London and Bristol, giving him an unusually sophisticated understanding of British commercial interests and vulnerabilities.

Laurens entered politics during the colonial resistance to the Stamp Act and the Townshend Acts, serving in the South Carolina Commons House of Assembly and gaining a reputation for principled firmness in constitutional argument. He was elected to the Continental Congress in 1777 and rose rapidly, his combination of commercial acumen, procedural rigor, and personal integrity making him the natural successor to Hancock when the latter resigned. He held the presidency for slightly over thirteen months, from November 1, 1777 to December 9, 1778, one of the longer and more consequential terms of the Continental Congress's wartime history.

## The Conway Cabal and Congressional Unity

When Laurens assumed the congressional presidency in November 1777, the institution was under severe internal strain. The Conway Cabal — a loosely organized faction involving General Thomas Conway, General Horatio Gates (the victor at Saratoga), and other officers and politicians who questioned Washington's military competence — was then at its height. Laurens navigated this crisis with characteristic directness: he was one of the few congressional leaders who communicated its details to Washington through his son John Laurens, who served as an aide-de-camp on Washington's staff. Laurens's transparency with Washington helped the commander-in-chief understand the dimensions of the threat against him and respond decisively, and the Cabal had collapsed by February 1778, during the Valley Forge encampment.

## Rejection of the Carlisle Peace Commission

The most politically consequential act of Laurens's congressional presidency was the formal rejection of the Carlisle Peace Commission's peace overtures. Britain dispatched the Commission — led by Frederick Howard, Earl of Carlisle, along with William Eden and George Johnstone — with authorization under the North Ministry's Conciliatory Acts (passed March 1778) to offer Americans self-governance and the repeal of the disputed taxes, short of independence. The Commission's proposals arrived in Philadelphia on June 13, 1778, two weeks after the French had formally committed to the alliance and after British forces under General Henry Clinton had already begun evacuating Philadelphia in preparation for the march to New York.

Laurens organized Congress's response with meticulous attention to proper form. The formal reply of June 17, 1778 was carefully drafted by committee, voted upon by the full Congress, and dispatched through official channels. It made Congress's conditions unambiguous: Britain must first recognize American independence or withdraw all British forces and fleet from American waters before any negotiations could begin. This response — courteous in language but absolute in substance — sent a decisive signal to both Britain and France. It demonstrated that Congress, having concluded the French alliance in February, would not fracture under negotiating pressure and that American war aims had hardened irreversibly to full independence. The commission departed for Britain in November 1778 having achieved nothing.

## Articles of Confederation Ratification

Laurens guided the state-by-state ratification of the Articles of Confederation through the critical year of 1778. Congress had approved the Articles on November 15, 1777, and they were sent immediately to the states. During Laurens's presidency, eleven of the thirteen states ratified: Virginia (December 16, 1777), South Carolina (February 5, 1778), New York (February 6, 1778), Rhode Island (February 9, 1778), Georgia (February 26, 1778), Connecticut (February 27, 1778), New Hampshire (March 4, 1778), Pennsylvania (March 5, 1778), Massachusetts (March 10, 1778), North Carolina (April 5, 1778), and New Jersey (November 26, 1778). Delaware and Maryland withheld ratification, both insisting that states with western land claims (Virginia, Massachusetts, Connecticut) cede those claims to Congress before a federal union could justly be formed. This dispute delayed full ratification until March 1, 1781, when Maryland became the thirteenth and final state to sign.

Despite the holdouts, Laurens's management of the ratification process through 1778 established the practical framework for federal governance: Congress operated as though the Articles were in effect, and the accumulating state ratifications gave it increasing institutional legitimacy. The constitutional architecture that would govern the United States through the end of the war owed substantially to the administrative steadiness Laurens maintained during his tenure.

## Resignation and the Deane-Lee Affair

Laurens's departure from the congressional presidency on December 9, 1778 was triggered by the Silas Deane controversy — one of the bitterest internal disputes of the Revolutionary period. Silas Deane, one of the Paris commissioners who had helped negotiate the French alliance, was recalled by Congress in August 1778 amid accusations by Arthur Lee (another commissioner) that he had enriched himself through fraudulent supply contracts. Laurens was drawn into the controversy because he believed Deane's accounts were confused and that the full truth had not emerged; when Congress appeared to be moving toward exonerating Deane without full investigation, Laurens submitted a formal letter of resignation, citing his inability to sign a resolution he believed was premature. His successor, John Jay, assumed the presidency on December 10, 1778.

## Capture, Imprisonment, and Peace Mission

After his congressional tenure, Laurens was elected minister plenipotentiary to the Netherlands in October 1779, charged with negotiating a Dutch loan for the American war effort. He sailed in August 1780 but was captured on August 3, 1780 by HMS Vestal in the North Atlantic. The British found in his luggage a draft treaty of amity between the United States and the Netherlands — a document that Britain used as a pretext to declare war on the Dutch Republic in December 1780. Laurens was transported to England and imprisoned in the Tower of London, becoming the first American to be held there. He was released on bail in December 1781 and was formally exchanged on February 13, 1782 in a prisoner exchange for Lord Cornwallis — a recognition of Laurens's symbolic importance to both sides.

Freed, Laurens joined Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, and John Jay as a peace commissioner in Paris, arriving in November 1782 in time to participate in the final round of negotiations. He contributed particularly to the article providing for the evacuation of British troops without the seizure of American property (including enslaved people claimed as property by their enslavers), a clause that became one of the most contentious provisions of the Treaty of Paris of September 3, 1783.

## Significance

Laurens's role as Congressional President in 1778 transformed the Continental Congress from a provisional and frequently chaotic body into an institution capable of definitive action. His handling of the Carlisle Commission — courteous but unambiguous in its rejection — sent a signal to both Britain and France that Congress would not splinter under negotiating pressure. This institutional standing became crucial when the Franco-American alliance needed validation through Congress; Laurens's formal authority and his demonstrated commitment to congressional unity made the alliance's ratification a matter of institutional consensus rather than partisan faction. The eleven-state ratification of the Articles of Confederation, shepherded through 1778, created the constitutional framework that governed the United States through the war.

Laurens's capture and imprisonment at the Tower of London added a personal dimension to his Revolutionary legacy — his exchange for Cornwallis underlined that both British and American governments viewed him as a figure of the first rank. His son John Laurens, Washington's aide and one of the war's most courageous young officers, died in a minor skirmish in South Carolina on August 27, 1782 — months before his father could return home — a loss Laurens described as the defining grief of his later life.
