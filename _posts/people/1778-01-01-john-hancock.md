**Key figures**: John Hancock, Samuel Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Henry Laurens

## Summary

John Hancock (January 23, 1737 – October 8, 1793) was a wealthy Boston merchant, leading patriot, and the most prominent political figure in Massachusetts during the American Revolution. His fortune, built on trade through the firm of Thomas Hancock & Co., made him one of New England's wealthiest men by his mid-twenties; it also made him a target of British customs enforcement. The 1768 seizure of his sloop *Liberty* on smuggling charges by royal customs officers sparked riots in Boston and transformed him into a symbol of colonial resistance.

### President of the Continental Congress, 1775–1777

Hancock was elected President of the Continental Congress on May 24, 1775, six weeks after the opening shots at Lexington and Concord. He presided over the body's most consequential decisions: the appointment of George Washington as Commander-in-Chief in June 1775, the formal adoption of the Declaration of Independence on August 2, 1776 (with Hancock signing first, in the large, bold hand that became legendary), and the drafting of the Articles of Confederation submitted to the states in November 1777. He served as President until October 29, 1777, when he resigned citing poor health and family obligations.

### 1778: Return to Congress and the Rhode Island Expedition

Hancock rejoined the Continental Congress in June 1778, hoping to reclaim the presidency he had left the previous autumn, but found Henry Laurens installed in the chair. His return was brief and unhappy. He was among the Massachusetts delegates who signed the Articles of Confederation in 1778, but he soon left Congress for military service.

During the summer of 1778, Hancock nominally commanded a force of some 6,000 Massachusetts militia in the joint Franco-American expedition against Newport, Rhode Island—an operation designed to exploit the new alliance and seize the British garrison there—though he left the planning and orders to professional soldiers. The Battle of Rhode Island (August 29, 1778) ended inconclusively when a storm scattered the French fleet under the Comte d'Estaing before it could provide decisive naval support, and the militia largely dispersed as the American contingent withdrew. The episode underscored the coordination difficulties of the new Franco-American alliance.

Hancock then returned to Massachusetts politics, winning election as the state's first governor under its new constitution in 1780—an office he would hold, with one interruption, until 1785 and again from 1787 until his death in 1793.

### Financial and Logistical Role

Throughout 1778, Hancock remained critical to the war effort through his personal financial resources and his network of Boston mercantile connections. Congress faced chronic shortages of specie, and wealthy patriots like Hancock were repeatedly called upon to advance funds or guarantee credit for military supplies. The Continental system relied heavily on such informal financial support alongside the increasingly depreciated Continental currency.

## Significance

Hancock's famous signature on the Declaration of Independence—reportedly made large enough for King George III to read without his spectacles—became one of the most recognizable images of American defiance. His 1778 participation in the Rhode Island expedition demonstrated a willingness to move beyond ceremonial politics into active military participation, reflecting the era's expectation that political leaders would share battlefield risk.

His governorship of Massachusetts (1780–1793, with gaps) gave him practical experience translating revolutionary principles into constitutional governance, including the challenge of suppressing Shays' Rebellion in 1786–1787. The Massachusetts Constitution of 1780, which he helped bring into force, was the world's first constitution drafted by a convention elected specifically for that purpose—a procedural innovation that influenced the 1787 Federal Convention.
