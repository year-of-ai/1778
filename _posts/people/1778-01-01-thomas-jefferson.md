**Key figures**: Thomas Jefferson, James Madison, Patrick Henry, George Wythe

## Summary

Thomas Jefferson (April 13, 1743 – July 4, 1826) entered 1778 as a Virginia House of Delegates member at age 34, immersed in the constitutional and legal reform of his home state. Though less visible on the international stage than fellow diplomats [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin/' | relative_url }}) or John Adams, Jefferson's 1778 work in Virginia's legislature proved consequential for American political thought and practice. Between 1776 and 1779, he drafted 126 bills over three years, transforming Virginia's legal inheritance from colonial aristocracy into a legal framework for republican equality.

Jefferson's legislative agenda targeted what he termed "feudal and unnatural distinctions" — the inherited privileges that concentrated land and political power in the hands of the wealthiest families. He worked to abolish entail (which restricted the sale of inherited estates to eldest sons in perpetuity) and primogeniture (the law assigning entire estates to the eldest son), opening Virginia's land market to broader ownership and diffusing dynastic wealth concentration. Simultaneously, he championed comprehensive educational reform, viewing public education as foundational to self-governance and an informed citizenry capable of preserving republican institutions. His most celebrated legislative effort — the Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom, which prohibited state support for religious institutions and the enforcement of religious orthodoxy — reflected Enlightenment principles of conscience and reason that aligned Jefferson's thought with the philosophical underpinnings of the Revolution itself.

## The 126-Bill Drafting Campaign, 1776–1779

Jefferson's legislative project was systematic rather than piecemeal. Working with a committee of revisors that included George Mason and Edmund Pendleton, Jefferson took the bulk of the drafting work upon himself, composing bills that addressed criminal law, inheritance, education, religion, and civic organization across Virginia's legal code. The 126 bills represented a complete overhaul of colonial Virginia's legal inheritance — colonial statutes and English common law assumptions that Jefferson believed were incompatible with republican self-governance.

Among the most consequential bills drafted in this period:

- **Bill to Abolish Entail** (passed 1776): eliminated the practice of restricting land inheritance to the eldest son, opening Virginia's land market to sale and distribution. Jefferson considered this among his most important achievements, as it prevented the emergence of a hereditary landed aristocracy in the new republic.
- **Bill for Proportioning Crimes and Punishments** (drafted 1778, not passed in its original form): reduced the scope of capital punishment in Virginia from approximately 27 capital crimes under colonial law to two — treason and murder — reflecting Enlightenment penal philosophy influenced by Cesare Beccaria.
- **Bill for Establishing a Public Library** (1779): proposed the creation of a public lending library in Richmond, Virginia, as part of a broader vision of civic education.

The sheer volume of legislation — 126 bills over three years — testifies to Jefferson's conception of the Revolution not as a military event but as a comprehensive social and legal transformation. He would later cite the revision of Virginia's laws alongside the Declaration of Independence and the founding of the University of Virginia as the three achievements most worth recording on his tombstone.

## The Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom

Jefferson's Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom, drafted in 1777 and introduced to the Virginia legislature during the 1778–1779 session, articulated the most philosophically ambitious argument for church-state separation in the Revolutionary era. The bill's preamble declared that "Almighty God hath created the mind free" and that all attempts to compel religious belief through civil authority were "a departure from the plan of the holy author of our religion." The operative clauses prohibited the state from compelling attendance at any religious establishment, from imposing taxes for the support of religion, and from penalizing citizens for their religious beliefs or lack thereof.

Patrick Henry's opposition represented the bill's most formidable obstacle in 1778 and through the early 1780s. Henry favored a "general assessment" — a state tax distributed among Christian denominations — rather than Jefferson's complete disestablishment. The bill stalled in the legislature throughout Jefferson's tenure, but James Madison revived and championed it after Jefferson departed for France in 1784. The Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom passed in January 1786, three years before the First Amendment codified the same principle for the federal Constitution.

The 1778 struggle over the bill exemplifies Jefferson's characteristic legislative method: drafting at the highest level of philosophical principle, creating a document that survived immediate defeat to shape future law. The bill's influence on [religious developments across the Revolutionary era]({{ '/news/politics/religious-developments-1778/' | relative_url }}) extended well beyond Virginia, providing a model for the constitutional provisions later championed by Madison at the Philadelphia Convention.

## Abolishing Entail and Primogeniture

Jefferson's elimination of the entail system, completed in 1776, and his efforts to abolish primogeniture (completed in 1785 after his departure for France) constituted what he called an "abolition of aristocracy." By breaking up the great tidewater estates through open inheritance, he sought to prevent Virginia from reproducing the class stratification of English landed society. The legislation opened land previously locked in family entails to sale, purchase, and division — an economic democratization that also had political consequences, as Virginia's property-owning electorate broadened as land became more accessible.

These bills directly complemented the broader national [Articles of Confederation ratification process]({{ '/news/politics/articles-of-confederation-1778/' | relative_url }}) by ensuring that the republican framework being constructed at the national level corresponded to the social conditions being deliberately created at the state level. Jefferson's vision was of interlocking reforms: legal equality produced the social conditions, public education produced the informed citizens, and religious freedom produced the moral autonomy that republican self-governance required.

## Educational Reform Vision

Jefferson's Bill for the More General Diffusion of Knowledge, drafted during his 1778 legislative work, proposed the most comprehensive public education system yet articulated in American politics. The bill envisioned a three-tier system: elementary schools providing universal literacy across Virginia's counties; district grammar schools providing secondary education, with scholarships for talented boys from poor families; and one college (William and Mary, which Jefferson sought to reform) providing advanced education for the most capable. The bill was never passed in Jefferson's lifetime, but it established the intellectual framework for American public education debates through the nineteenth century.

Jefferson's educational vision was inseparable from his political theory: a republic's stability depended on a broadly literate and informed electorate capable of recognizing and resisting tyranny. Without education, democratic institutions were empty forms. This conviction, elaborated in his 1778 legislative drafts, shaped his later founding of the University of Virginia (established 1819) — the project he considered the culmination of his educational reform program.

## Jefferson and the Solar Eclipse of 1778

Among Jefferson's activities during 1778 was his attempt to observe the [total solar eclipse of June 24, 1778]({{ '/news/science/solar-eclipse-1778/' | relative_url }}), which crossed the southeastern colonies. Jefferson traveled to or prepared instruments at his Monticello estate in Virginia for the observation, working in coordination with other American natural philosophers attempting to record the eclipse's path. Though clouds partly obscured his view, his attempt to observe the eclipse reflects Jefferson's lifelong engagement with natural philosophy — he maintained meteorological records, corresponded with scientific societies in Europe, and considered scientific observation an obligation of the educated republican citizen.

Jefferson's scientific interests connected him to the broader [Enlightenment network of 1778]({{ '/news/arts/enlightenment-transition-1778/' | relative_url }}), in which natural philosophy and political philosophy were understood as aspects of the same project of rational inquiry and human improvement. His correspondence with figures like David Rittenhouse — Philadelphia's foremost astronomical observer, who successfully recorded the [eclipse from the city]({{ '/news/science/david-rittenhouse/' | relative_url }}) — placed Jefferson within the transatlantic republic of letters even while he was engaged in state legislative work.

## Transition to Governor

In June 1779, immediately following the culmination of his 1778–1779 legislative session, Jefferson was elected Governor of Virginia by the House of Delegates, succeeding Patrick Henry for successive one-year terms in 1779 and 1780. As governor, he implemented several of the reforms he had drafted as a delegate, including the relocation of Virginia's capital from colonial Williamsburg to Richmond — a symbolic shift away from tidewater planter culture toward the interior and the frontier, completing a geographic democratization that paralleled his legal reforms. His governorship concluded in 1781 as British forces invaded Virginia under Benedict Arnold and later Lord Cornwallis, forcing Jefferson to evacuate Charlottesville and briefly flee Monticello itself — a humiliation he never fully escaped in political life.

## Significance

Jefferson's 1778 legislative work in Virginia established him as a principal theorist of republican government at the crucial moment when the Revolution was transitioning from military conflict to constitutional governance. His Bills for Judicial Reform, Education, and Religious Freedom articulated principles — popular sovereignty, separation of church and state, universal education — that would shape American constitutional development and influence democratic movements worldwide. Though many of his 1778 bills failed to pass immediately, they preserved in written form the philosophical commitments of the Revolutionary generation and provided templates for reform that Madison and others would implement in subsequent years and decades.

Jefferson's 1778 work ran in parallel to [Edmund Burke's parliamentary opposition]({{ '/news/politics/edmund-burke-parliamentary-opposition/' | relative_url }}) in Britain, which was simultaneously articulating the grounds for recognizing American independence. Together, Burke in Westminster and Jefferson in Williamsburg were constructing, from opposite sides of the Atlantic, the theoretical framework for a political settlement that would eventually end the war.
