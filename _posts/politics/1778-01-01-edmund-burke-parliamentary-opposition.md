**Key figures**: Edmund Burke, Charles James Fox, Charles Watson-Wentworth (2nd Marquess of Rockingham), Lord North, King George III

## Summary

Edmund Burke had opposed coercive British policy toward the American colonies since the mid-1770s, delivering his *Speech on American Taxation* (1774) and *Speech on Conciliation with the Colonies* (1775) in failed attempts to persuade Parliament toward accommodation. By 1778, with Burgoyne's surrender at Saratoga (October 1777) behind them and the [Franco-American Alliance]({{ '/news/politics/franco-american-alliance/' | relative_url }}) of February 6, 1778 transforming the conflict into a world war, Burke and the Rockingham Whigs concluded that reconciliation on terms short of independence was no longer possible. In the spring of 1778, Burke argued in the House of Commons that the peace commissioners about to be dispatched to America — the [Carlisle Peace Commission]({{ '/news/politics/carlisle-peace-commission/' | relative_url }}) — should be granted explicit authority to recognize American independence outright, a position more advanced than the government's offer of home rule within the empire.

Burke's opposition gained a decisive ally in 1778 when Charles James Fox formally joined the Rockingham Whigs, beginning one of the most consequential political partnerships of the era. Together, Burke and Fox attacked Lord North's ministry in Parliament for having mismanaged relations with France to the point of driving Paris into open alliance with the rebellious colonies, and for a war they regarded as both unwinnable and unjust. Burke also publicly objected to the government's strategy of enlisting Native American allies to raid frontier settlements — the same frontier warfare carried out by [Haudenosaunee-allied forces]({{ '/news/military/haudenosaunee-strategic-alliance/' | relative_url }}) at Wyoming and Cherry Valley that summer and autumn — and to policies encouraging revolt among enslaved people in the southern colonies, arguing both policies were morally indefensible escalations of the conflict.

Burke's 1778 activity was not confined to American policy. In May 1778 he championed parliamentary bills easing restrictions on Irish trade, resisting fierce pressure from his own Bristol merchant constituents and publishing *Two Letters to Gentlemen of Bristol on the Bills Relative to the Trade of Ireland* in their defense. That same session he helped draft the preamble for what became the Catholic Relief Act 1778 (the Papists Act), which received royal assent on June 3, 1778 and relaxed penal restrictions on Catholics in England — a further break with his Bristol electorate's wishes.

## Burke's Parliamentary Record in 1778: Key Interventions

Burke's speeches in the 1778 parliamentary session were among the most active of his career. In February he responded to the news of the Franco-American Alliance with a detailed indictment of North's handling of relations with Versailles, arguing that the [Comte de Vergennes]({{ '/news/people/comte-de-vergennes/' | relative_url }}) had been handed an unnecessary victory by British inflexibility: "We have done everything to drive France into this war except provoke her formally, and then we have done that too." The speech was not printed in authorized form but was reconstructed from parliamentary reporters' notes and circulated widely.

In March and April, as the [Carlisle Peace Commission]({{ '/news/politics/carlisle-peace-commission/' | relative_url }}) was being constituted, Burke pressed for a commitment to recognize independence as the prerequisite for any meaningful negotiation. Lord North's ministry rejected this, limiting the Commission's mandate to offers of home rule and taxation concessions — terms Burke correctly predicted would be rejected by the Continental Congress. The Commission's failure, confirmed when Congress refused even to meet with its members in June 1778, vindicated Burke's assessment in the eyes of Rockingham Whig supporters.

Burke's objection to Native American alliance is documented in correspondence and in reported parliamentary interventions. He wrote to the Marquess of Rockingham in the summer of 1778 that the use of Haudenosaunee warriors represented a form of warfare incompatible with civilized precedent — language that reflected his broader philosophical hostility to unconventional escalation, later articulated in the *Reflections on the Revolution in France* (1790). His critique appeared in Parliament weeks after the Wyoming massacre became known in London, adding specific and damning evidence to a general argument he had been making for two years.

## The Fox–Burke Alliance: Origins and Political Consequences

Charles James Fox had broken with the North ministry in 1774 over American taxation but had not been consistently aligned with the Rockinghams. His formal adhesion to their group in 1778 marked a significant realignment: Fox brought oratorical brilliance, a gambling-and-gaming-house network of personal supporters, and — crucially — a direct relationship with the Prince of Wales that gave the opposition a foothold in the court. Together with Burke's philosophical and historical arguments, Fox's political energy made the Rockingham Whigs, for the first time, a credible alternative government rather than a permanent minority faction.

The alliance also had costs. Fox's personal extravagance and reputation for vice made him an easy target for North's supporters; Burke's Bristol constituents viewed the Fox–Burke pairing as evidence that their member had abandoned commercial common sense for aristocratic faction politics. The Irish trade bills and Catholic Relief Act confirmed these suspicions, and by 1780, when Bristol's electors forced Burke to stand down rather than face almost-certain defeat, the political damage of 1778's principled stands had accumulated to an irreversible degree.

## Irish Trade Bills and Catholic Relief Act, 1778

The Irish trade legislation Burke championed in 1778 was economically significant: Bristol merchants feared that easing restrictions on Irish import of wool, glass, and certain manufactured goods would undercut English manufacturers and reduce the competitive advantage their city held through imperial preferential trade. Burke's counter-argument — that Ireland's economic integration with Britain was more valuable than the narrow protection of Bristol's industries, and that starving Ireland of trade would produce disaffection useful to Britain's enemies — was a sophisticated mercantilist argument at odds with his constituents' immediate interests.

The Catholic Relief Act 1778, which received royal assent on June 3, repealed parts of the 1698 Papists Act that had prohibited Catholics from inheriting or purchasing land and from keeping schools. The relief was modest in scope but symbolically significant, and it provoked a backlash that culminated two years later in the Gordon Riots of June 1780, when anti-Catholic crowds burned Catholic chapels and properties across London. Burke, who had supported the Act from its drafting, was personally threatened during the riots; the episode reinforced his evolving conviction that unreasoned popular passion — "the swinish multitude" — posed a fundamental danger to constitutional order. This thread runs directly into his 1790 *Reflections*, making the 1778 Catholic relief episode a biographical turning point as well as a legislative one.

[Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin/' | relative_url }}), negotiating in Paris through 1778, was aware of Burke's parliamentary positions through diplomatic and press channels; Vergennes's agents monitored British parliamentary debates closely as intelligence on the resilience of North's government. Burke's visible dissent was factored into French assessments that the ministry might not survive a prolonged war — an assessment that reinforced France's decision to commit fully to American alliance rather than seeking a separate peace.

## Significance

Burke's 1778 record marks the definitive point at which the parliamentary opposition abandoned reconciliation for recognition of American independence, hardening the Whig critique of the war into the position it would hold until the conflict's end. The Fox–Burke alliance forged that year became the organizing axis of opposition politics for the following decade. Burke's principled but unpopular stands — on Irish trade, Catholic relief, and the conduct of the war — cost him dearly: Bristol's electors, angered on all three counts, forced him to abandon his seat there in 1780.

His objection to Britain's use of Native American allies on the frontier anticipated and gave political voice to the same year's escalating warfare in the Haudenosaunee homeland, where British-allied raids devastated Wyoming and Cherry Valley. The [Global Crisis of 1778]({{ '/news/politics/global-crisis-of-1778/' | relative_url }}) framing captures how Burke's domestic opposition was inseparable from the international transformation of the war — he understood, better than most in Westminster, that France's entry had fundamentally changed what Britain was fighting for and what it could realistically hope to achieve.
