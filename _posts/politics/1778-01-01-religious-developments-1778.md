**Key figures**: Pope Pius VI, Joseph II of the Holy Roman Empire, Thomas Jefferson, Louis XVI of France

## Summary

The year 1778 represented a watershed moment in the relationship between religious and secular power. Pope Pius VI, now in his fourth year as head of the Catholic Church, navigated an increasingly assertive secular order that sought to subordinate ecclesiastical authority to state interests. Simultaneously, in America and Britain's dissenting movements, religious liberty emerged as a revolutionary principle, challenging established churches' monopolies on Christian practice and state sponsorship. The tensions between these developments defined the religious politics of 1778.

Pope Pius VI ascended to the papacy on February 15, 1775, taking office at a pivotal moment. His election itself had been contentious: Spain, Portugal, and France initially opposed him, suspecting him of favoring the Jesuits—a preference that proved prescient when Pius VI later attempted, unsuccessfully, to check the suppression of the Society of Jesus that had begun under his predecessor. In 1778, Pius VI achieved a significant diplomatic success by concluding a satisfactory concordat with Portugal following the accession of Queen Maria I in 1777. This agreement represented the papacy's ability to negotiate with secular powers on territorial and ecclesiastical matters, even as those same powers eroded papal authority in other domains.

However, Pius VI faced far more serious challenges from enlightened monarchs who viewed religious authority as a threat to state sovereignty. The most aggressive assertion of state over ecclesiastical power came from Joseph II of the Holy Roman Empire, whose reforms were aimed at subordinating the Church to state interests in the name of enlightened rationalism. Joseph II claimed the right to nominate ecclesiastical beneficiaries, control papal communications within his domains, and regulate monastic life. In Spain, Sardinia, and Venice, governments followed Joseph's precedent, curtailing papal jurisdiction and subordinating bishops to royal authority. The Two Sicilies pursued the most sweeping anti-ecclesiastical reforms: King Ferdinand IV of Naples refused to recognize papal briefs without royal approval and arrogated to the crown the exclusive right to appoint bishops and abbots. These reforms presaged what would become widespread across Catholic Europe—the subordination of religious to secular power as a defining feature of enlightened despotism.

In America, the Revolutionary War catalyzed religious reform in an opposite direction: toward the separation of religious from state power not to subordinate the Church, but to guarantee religious liberty. During 1777–1778, Thomas Jefferson drafted the Virginia Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom, which attacked the colonial legacy of religious establishment and compulsory church taxation. Virginia's 1776 Declaration of Rights had already proclaimed the "free exercise of religion," but the Church of England remained legally established, with tax revenues funding its ministers and compulsory attendance still nominally enforced. Jefferson's draft statute, introduced to the Virginia General Assembly in 1779, went further: it proposed to disestablish the Church of England entirely and guarantee freedom of conscience to Christians, Jews, and dissenters of all faiths. The statute declared that "No man shall be compelled to frequent or support any worship, place, or ministry whatsoever," a revolutionary affirmation that would not become law until 1786 but expressed the religious liberty principle that would shape the First Amendment.

The movement for religious toleration in Britain's dissenting communities reinforced American developments. British Dissenters—Presbyterians, Congregationalists, Baptists, and others excluded from the established Anglican Church—demanded equal civil rights and relief from discriminatory taxes and restrictions. The Toleration Act of 1689 had granted limited freedom to Protestant dissenters, but Catholics remained severely restricted, and even Protestant dissenters faced test acts preventing them from holding public office. The broader Enlightenment argument—advanced by figures like Jefferson, Voltaire, and Montesquieu—held that coerced religion corrupted faith, that religious diversity strengthened civil society, and that state neutrality on doctrine preserved both religious sincerity and political stability.

Across Catholic Europe, the Enlightenment assault on ecclesiastical privilege intensified. The Austrian Habsburg domains experienced particular ferment. Joseph II, having already consolidated state control over bishoprics and monastery lands, prepared the sweep of church reforms that would explode in the 1780s Edict of Toleration. The Jesuits, historically the papacy's most reliable ally in the Counter-Reformation, faced suppression across Catholic Europe: suppressed by Portugal (1759), France (1764), Spain (1767), and finally by papal order itself under Pope Clement XIV in 1773, though Pope Pius VI harbored sympathy for the order. The Society's demise symbolized the papacy's inability to prevent secular states from dismantling even the most powerful ecclesiastical institution.

In France, where the king still commanded considerable ecclesiastical power through his role as "Most Christian Majesty," the Enlightenment debate over religious toleration gained political salience. The legal disabilities of French Protestants—forbidden to worship openly, barred from professions, subject to brutal persecution—stood in sharp contrast to the theorists' arguments for toleration. While Louis XVI, unlike his predecessor Louis XV, was personally pious and protective of Church interests, he was also exposed to Enlightenment arguments through the philosophes at court. In 1787—just years after 1778—Louis XVI would issue the Edict of Versailles, restoring civil rights to French non-Catholics, though not yet granting them freedom of worship. This hesitant step toward toleration reflected the tensions between absolute monarchy's need for social order and the Enlightenment's insistence on religious liberty.

## Virginia's Religious Freedom Debate in 1778

Of all the American states, Virginia in 1778 stood at the sharpest intersection of religious establishment and revolutionary principle. Virginia's 1776 Declaration of Rights, drafted primarily by George Mason, had proclaimed "that religion, or the duty which we owe to our Creator, and the manner of discharging it, can be directed only by reason and conviction, not by force or violence; and therefore all men are equally entitled to the free exercise of religion, according to the dictates of conscience." Yet the Church of England remained established in Virginia law: clergymen received public salaries funded by taxes on all residents, Anglican vestries controlled poor relief and vital records, and the church's institutional infrastructure remained legally privileged.

Thomas Jefferson's draft of the Virginia Statute for Establishing Religious Freedom circulated in intellectual circles throughout 1777–1778 as he refined its philosophical foundations. Jefferson grounded the statute in Lockean epistemology — the argument that religious belief, being a matter of reasoned conviction rather than public conduct, was outside the legitimate scope of state coercion. His draft proclaimed: "Almighty God hath created the mind free; that all attempts to influence it by temporal punishments or burthens, or by civil incapacitations, tend only to beget habits of hypocrisy and meanness." Jefferson formally introduced the statute in the Virginia General Assembly in June 1779, where it languished until James Madison maneuvered it through in 1786.

The Virginia debate in 1778 took place against a practical backdrop: wartime conditions had effectively dismantled Anglican establishment without legislation. Anglican clergy, many of whom had been trained in England and remained loyal to the Crown, departed or remained silent. Congregations dissolved. The vestry system, already weakened by wartime disruption, ceased to function in many parishes. Virginia's Presbyterians, Baptists, and Quakers — whose dissenting status had made them second-class subjects under Anglican establishment — used the war to argue forcefully for permanent disestablishment. Baptist petitions flooded the General Assembly in 1777–1779 demanding an end to compulsory church taxation. These grassroots religious liberty claims drove the legislative debate as much as Jefferson's philosophical statute.

Patrick Henry, by contrast, proposed in 1784 a "General Assessment" bill that would have required all Virginians to pay a tax supporting "teachers of the Christian religion" — a pluralistic establishment that would fund multiple denominations rather than only Anglicans. Madison's 1785 "Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments" defeated Henry's bill and cleared the way for Jefferson's statute. The contest between Jefferson-Madison separationism and Henry's pluralistic establishment structured not only Virginia's 1778–1786 debate but anticipates the First Amendment's "establishment clause" and "free exercise clause" distinctions.

## The Papal Response to Enlightened Despotism

Pius VI's strategy in 1778 was one of strategic accommodation rather than confrontation. Unable to prevent secular encroachments on papal authority, he sought to maintain the papacy's diplomatic relevance through negotiated concordats. The Portuguese concordat of 1778 exemplified this approach: rather than risk conflict with secular powers, the papacy negotiated settlements that preserved its nominal authority while accepting significant state power over ecclesiastical appointments and jurisdiction.

The Portuguese concordat's terms illustrate the papacy's declining position. Portugal under Queen Maria I — who succeeded the reformist Marquis of Pombal's patron José I in February 1777 — initially seemed friendlier to Rome than its predecessor. Pombal had expelled the Jesuits in 1759 and conducted a running conflict with the Holy See; Maria I dismissed Pombal within months of her accession and sought reconciliation with Rome. The 1778 concordat restored diplomatic relations severed under Pombal, but the settlement was on secular terms: Portugal retained the patronage rights to ecclesiastical appointments in its overseas territories and the right to nominate bishops, leaving the papacy with confirmatory rather than initiating authority. The papacy gained ceremonial dignity and diplomatic normalcy at the price of jurisdictional concession — a pattern repeated across Catholic Europe.

Joseph II's parallel campaign was less diplomatic and more coercive. His 1781 Patent of Toleration extended legal rights to Lutherans, Calvinists, and Greek Orthodox Christians in his dominions — while withholding full toleration from Jews and maintaining Catholic institutional dominance — and from 1782 he began dissolving monasteries that did not serve "useful" social functions such as education or hospital care. Pius VI traveled personally to Vienna in March 1782 to remonstrate with Joseph — an extraordinary papal journey undertaken for the first time since the Reformation — but Joseph received him courteously and ignored his objections. The papacy's theological authority remained intact; its political leverage over enlightened Catholic monarchs had effectively evaporated.

This pragmatism would define Pius VI's long papacy (1775–1799) and would be severely tested during the French Revolution, when the papacy faced existential challenges to its temporal and spiritual authority.

The Congress of Ems, convened by the German ecclesiastical princes in 1786, represented the organized ecclesiastical rebellion against the papacy in the name of state supremacy and Enlightenment reform. The princes' attempt to assert episcopal independence from Rome and subordinate the Church to state councils foreshadowed the National Church movement during the French Revolution. Though the Congress postdated 1778, it emerged from the decade of tension that 1778 exemplified.

## Voltaire's Final Year and the Enlightenment's Religious Legacy

The death of Voltaire on May 30, 1778 — the same year that concentrated so many religious-political pressures — provided symbolic punctuation to the religious developments of the period. Voltaire had spent decades attacking religious intolerance, superstition, and clerical power under the rallying cry *Écrasez l'infâme* ("Crush the infamous thing"), widely understood as a call to destroy fanaticism and clerical tyranny. His plays, philosophical tales, and historical writings had shaped educated European opinion on religious liberty more profoundly than any single political act. The Catholic Church refused him a Christian burial in Paris, forcing his body to be secretly transported to Champagne for interment. When his remains were moved to the Panthéon in 1791, the ceremony became a revolutionary tableau — the philosopher of religious liberty enshrined as a founding ancestor of the new secular republic. The religious debates of 1778 cannot be separated from Voltaire's lifelong campaign, which had made religious liberty intellectually respectable among the ruling classes of Europe and America alike.

## Significance

The religious developments of 1778 embodied a fundamental shift in the relationship between religious and political authority. The old Christendom model—in which secular and ecclesiastical power were interwoven, with the papacy claiming ultimate spiritual authority—fractured under enlightened despotism and Revolutionary republicanism. In Europe, secular states claimed effective control over ecclesiastical structures, transforming bishops into royal servants and churches into tools of state policy. In America, revolutionaries proposed something theoretically more radical: a state that was neutral on religious questions, that guaranteed freedom of conscience, and that derived legitimacy from popular sovereignty rather than divine sanction. 

Pope Pius VI's early papacy marked the beginning of the end for the papacy's temporal power. By 1798, French Revolutionary armies would capture the Pope and abolish the Papal States, reducing the papacy to a territorial prisoner in Rome. But the intellectual groundwork for this catastrophe was laid during the 1770s–1780s, when enlightened despots and democratic revolutionaries alike challenged ecclesiastical privilege and asserted the supremacy of state or popular will. The religious liberty arguments advanced in Virginia and the dissenting circles of Britain in 1778 would prove more enduring than either enlightened despotism or papal restoration: they shaped the modern conception of religious freedom that emerged from the American and French Revolutions.
