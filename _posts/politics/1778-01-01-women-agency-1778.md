The year 1778 is conventionally narrated through the diplomacy of kings, the strategies of generals, and the rhetoric of philosophers. Yet beneath and alongside this masculine political sphere, women of the American colonies, France, and Europe exercised agency and influence that proved as consequential to the period's outcome as the treaties and battles that dominate the historical record. Their power took multiple forms: the economic power to enforce patriot boycotts; the political power to gather intelligence and shape opinion; the intellectual power to document events and posterity; the social power to move salons and shape court favor; and the symbolic power to embody national aspiration or decline.

## American Women and Economic-Political Power

The American Revolution created an unexpected opening for women's political voice. Though legally excluded from voting, holding office, and direct political participation, women discovered that their role as household managers—long rendered invisible and politically irrelevant—could become a site of political agency. Beginning in the mid-1770s with boycotts of British goods, women organized the domestic refusal that made British commercial sanctions bite: they forsook British tea, spun cloth locally to replace British imports, and (crucially) used their purchasing power and social approval to enforce patriot discipline. Young women refused the company of men who violated boycott agreements and severed relationships with those who failed to sign patriotic associations. These were not trivial social gestures; they weaponized the emotional and social stakes of courtship and marriage to enforce political conformity.

By 1778, this economic power had proven decisive. The continental blockade was not maintained by the Continental Navy (which scarcely existed) but by the aggregate effect of thousands of women's household purchasing decisions, enforced by the shame and social ostracism of their peers. As one observer noted, "females" had become "the driving force behind boycotts," transforming what might appear as housewifery into political action of strategic consequence.

## Abigail Adams and Mercy Otis Warren: Intellectual Networks and Political Correspondence

Two women in particular emerged as intellectual architects of American political thought during the Revolutionary period. [Abigail Adams]({{ '/news/people/abigail-adams/' | relative_url }}), managing the Adams household and finances while her husband John Adams negotiated French alliances, maintained an extensive wartime correspondence that recorded, analyzed, and critiqued events swirling through the colonies. [Mercy Otis Warren]({{ '/news/people/mercy-otis-warren/' | relative_url }}), the poet, playwright, and satirist of Boston's revolutionary circles, deployed her literary talent to shape public opinion through political verse and drama—forms that allowed women to speak to matters of state while maintaining the guise of artistic expression.

In 1775, the Massachusetts General Court itself entrusted Adams and Warren (along with Hannah Winthrop) with an official political duty: to question fellow Massachusetts women who had remained loyal to the British crown. This official deputization, though limited in scope, represents a remarkable acknowledgment of women's political standing during crisis. The two women maintained a lifelong friendship and intellectual partnership, exchanging books and ideas through a female literary network that paralleled and complemented the male political networks from which women were formally barred. Their correspondence and published writings became, for later historians, the principal record of how women *understood* and *experienced* the Revolution—not merely as spectators, but as conscious political actors.

## Mercy Otis Warren and Political Satire

By 1778, Mercy Otis Warren had already published political satires that lampooned British policy and royal pretense. Though her plays were rarely staged—women's public performance was culturally taboo—her literary voice reached Boston's educated circles through manuscript circulation and private readings. In October 1778, she published anonymous political verse in the *Boston Gazette* critiquing wartime profiteering, turning personal grief (her brother James Otis Jr. had suffered a mental breakdown during the Revolution) into public argument about the Revolution's meaning. Her pen became an instrument of political judgment and moral witness.

## The Battlefield and Beyond: Women Under Arms

At the [Battle of Monmouth]({{ '/news/military/battle-of-monmouth/' | relative_url }}) on June 28, 1778, the woman known as "Molly Pitcher" (likely [Mary Ludwig Hays]({{ '/news/military/battle-of-monmouth/' | relative_url }})) carried water and ammunition to the Continental artillery crew, replacing her wounded husband at the cannon. Her presence on the battlefield—whether fighting openly or in disguise—was neither exceptional nor unique. Women served in the ranks during the Revolutionary War, some discovered and discharged, others remaining undetected through the war's duration. The Molly Pitcher legend would later obscure the prosaic fact that women had been present at American battles from the earliest engagements, not as camp followers alone but as active combatants.

## European Women: Courts, Salons, and Power

Across the Atlantic, European women were not merely observers of 1778's diplomatic transformations but participants in its power structures. Marie Antoinette, the twenty-three-year-old Queen of France in 1778, had endured years of court gossip about her infertility. In December 1778, after eight years of marriage, she gave birth to her first child, Marie Thérèse Charlotte. The political significance of this birth extended far beyond the nursery: it secured the succession, silenced critics who whispered that the young queen was unfit to fulfill her role, and positioned her as a mother-figure essential to the realm. Her naming of the child after her beloved sister, [Maria Carolina]({{ '/news/people/maria-carolina-naples/' | relative_url }})—herself the powerful regent of Naples—linked the two women as architects of a new generation's political destiny.

[Maria Carolina of Austria and Naples]({{ '/news/people/maria-carolina-naples/' | relative_url }}), Marie Antoinette's elder sister, had in 1778 ascended to effective co-rule of the Naples kingdom after bearing a male heir. While her brother-in-law [King Louis XVI]({{ '/news/people/louis-xvi/' | relative_url }}) decided France's alliance with America through Vergennes' counsel, Maria Carolina was implementing Enlightenment reforms, modernizing the Neapolitan navy, and orchestrating diplomacy that would eventually align Naples with the broader anti-British coalition. Her power was circumscribed by law and custom in ways different from but not less consequential than her sister's; she wielded the authority of a regent and the influence of a woman who had proven her fertility and her political acumen.

## Sophie Arnould and the Power of Performance

In Paris's theatrical world, [Sophie Arnould]({{ '/news/arts/sophie-arnould/' | relative_url }}), the prima donna of the Opéra, commanded an influence that extended into the court and salon society. As the artistic centerpiece of the 1778 [Gluck–Piccinni Querelle]({{ '/news/arts/gluck-piccinni-querelle-1778/' | relative_url }})—the bitter operatic rivalry that divided Parisian taste—Arnould's vocal interpretations shaped not merely artistic fashion but social allegiance. Gluckists and Piccinnists formed rival factions in the salons and drawing rooms; to support Arnould's interpretations of Gluck heroines was to take a political stance about the nature of art, reason, and emotion. Her salon was a gathering place for intellectuals, aristocrats, and diplomats; her wit and opinions circulated through the gossip networks that paralleled and influenced the formal channels of statecraft.

## Salon Culture and Intellectual Networks

The salon culture that dominated Paris and Vienna in 1778 was overwhelmingly the creation of women. Women hosted the gatherings where philosophers, scientists, diplomats, and artists debated the meaning of Enlightenment, the justice of absolute monarchy, and the possibilities of political reform. These were not merely social events; they were the primary venues through which new ideas circulated, through which reputations were made and broken, and through which the intellectual foundations of the coming French Revolution were laid. [Benjamin Franklin]({{ '/news/people/benjamin-franklin/' | relative_url }}) himself participated in these salons, cultivating his image as the rustic American sage while gathering intelligence and securing support for the alliance. Women like the Marquise du Deffand (Marie Anne de Vichy-Chamrond) — though by 1778 in declining health, she died in September 1780 — had created and maintained the intellectual infrastructure through which Franklin's diplomacy became persuasive. Franklin's frequent attendance at the salon of Madame Helvétius in Auteuil was so well-documented that contemporaries joked he had found a second home in France — and his access to French elite opinion through these female-hosted networks was indispensable to his success in securing the alliance that [Louis XVI]({{ '/news/people/louis-xvi/' | relative_url }}) formalized on February 6, 1778.

## Correspondence Networks as Political Infrastructure

One of the least visible but most consequential forms of women's political power in 1778 was the management of correspondence networks. Long-distance communication was the lifeblood of revolutionary politics — battles, decisions, crises, and negotiations were narrated, analyzed, and argued through letters — and women were disproportionately responsible for maintaining these networks. Abigail Adams wrote an estimated 1,200 letters to John Adams over the course of the Revolution; Mercy Otis Warren maintained a literary correspondence that spanned both sides of the Atlantic. These women did not merely transmit information: they analyzed it, offered strategic judgments, and shaped their correspondents' understanding of events. Abigail Adams's famous 1776 injunction to John to "remember the ladies" is only the most celebrated instance of women using correspondence to stake a claim for political recognition. By 1778, with her husband in Paris negotiating the alliance with France, her letters document her acute awareness of [British strategy]({{ '/news/people/george-iii/' | relative_url }}) as it evolved and her private assessments of diplomatic prospects — assessments grounded in wide reading and active information-gathering, not mere domestic reportage.

The letters also functioned as memory. In an era before reliable newspapers reached the countryside, and before official archives were accessible to civilians, women's correspondence preserved the experiential record of the Revolution for posterity. Later historians of the 1778 period have depended heavily on women's letters to reconstruct the social and psychological texture of wartime life that official sources omit entirely.

## Epilogue: Invisible yet Decisive

The power wielded by women in 1778 was largely invisible to the official record and remains often invisible to historians. Abigail Adams's letters to John were not state documents; Mercy Otis Warren's anonymous verse did not carry formal authority; the household purchasing decisions of American women were not recorded in diplomatic cables; the children borne by queens and regents were recorded only as dynastic succession; the influence of salon hostesses was measured in gossip and favor, not in signed treaties. Yet the aggregate effect of these invisible powers was to shape the year profoundly.

The American Revolution succeeded not merely because of the Continental Army or French ships, but because women enforced the economic discipline of boycott and shaped the intellectual climate of resistance. French support for the American cause was not determined by kings alone, but by the salon networks in which support was cultivated and the court politics in which Marie Antoinette's bearing a son became consequential to dynastic stability. The future queens and regents of Europe—Maria Carolina, Marie Antoinette—were in 1778 consolidating the power that would make them political actors in their own right, even as formal institutions denied them the titles and offices held by men.

1778 thus witnessed not a single form of agency or influence, but many: some organized and collective (women's boycotts), some intellectual and solitary (Mercy Otis Warren's pen), some ceremonial and symbolic (queens bearing heirs), some performative and social (salon hostesses and opera singers). Together, these forms of power constituted a parallel political system that intersected with, influenced, and often constrained the masculine formal sphere documented in treaties and battle reports. To understand 1778 fully is to recognize that its great transformations were shaped by women as well as men—often invisibly, always consequentially.
