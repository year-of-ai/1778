## Summary

The ratification of the Articles of Confederation in 1778 represented the Continental Congress's attempt to codify the legal basis for American independence and war aims, even as the Franco-American alliance (signed February 6, 1778) was transforming the conflict into a global war. Congress had drafted and approved the Articles in November 1777 and submitted them to the states for ratification. Throughout 1778, ten states ratified the document in rapid succession—South Carolina, New York, Rhode Island, Connecticut, Georgia, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, Massachusetts, North Carolina, and New Jersey—each sealing its commitment to a federal union in which states retained individual sovereignty while delegating specified powers to Congress. 

However, two small states—Delaware and Maryland—refused to ratify, creating a constitutional impasse. The major stumbling block was Maryland's refusal to ratify until the landed states (particularly Virginia) agreed to cede their western land claims to the federal union. This dispute, rooted in conflicting territorial ambitions and the fear by land-poor states of domination by land-rich ones, delayed final ratification until March 1, 1781—nearly four years after Congressional approval and three years after the 1778 ratification surge. Nevertheless, 1778 represented the critical moment when ten of thirteen states committed to the Articles, establishing the legal and constitutional framework for American wartime governance and demonstrating to France that the Revolutionary cause rested on a genuine federal union, not merely a military coalition.

---

## Significance

**1. Constitutional Commitment Amid War**  
The 1778 ratifications demonstrated that the thirteen states, despite the military and economic pressures of the Revolutionary War, were willing to negotiate the terms of their union rather than devolve into thirteen separate sovereignties. Each state's ratification was an affirmation of American independence as a collective rather than purely individual project. The timing of the ratifications—concentrated in February through March 1778, just weeks after the Franco-American alliance was signed—showed that American statesmen viewed the Articles as essential to credibility with France. The French needed assurance that the American union possessed a legal framework and demonstrable commitment to coherence before investing its military and financial resources. The 1778 ratifications provided that assurance.

**2. The Principle of State Sovereignty**  
The Articles of Confederation embodied a revolutionary principle distinct from the constitutional settlement that would follow in 1787: each state retained absolute sovereignty except for those powers "expressly delegated" to Congress. This was a direct rejection of the British imperial model, which had concentrated power in Parliament and the Crown. By insisting on state sovereignty as the default, the Articles reflected the colonists' experience of imperial subordination and their determination never to be dominated by a centralized authority again. The principle was not merely theoretical—it had immediate practical consequences. Congress could declare war and make treaties (a power delegated to it), but it could not levy taxes or requisition troops with the authority of a sovereign nation-state. It had to request money and soldiers from the states, which retained the power to refuse. This asymmetry between Congress's formal authority and its practical dependence on state compliance would become a critical weakness during the war, contributing to the supply crisis at Valley Forge and the Continental dollar's collapse, even as the 1778 ratifications were still taking place.

**3. The Western Lands Dispute and Federalism**  
Maryland's refusal to ratify until Virginia and other land-claiming states ceded western claims to the federal union was not mere obstinacy—it represented a fundamental question about the nature of the union. Maryland (and Delaware and New Jersey) feared that states with vast western land claims—Virginia most prominently, but also New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and North Carolina—would accumulate disproportionate power and wealth by exploiting those territories independently. By refusing to ratify until the lands were federalized, Maryland forced a constitutional crisis that would not be resolved until 1781, when Virginia finally agreed to cede its claims. The dispute was a precursor to the 1787 constitutional crisis over representation and federalism; it demonstrated that the fundamental question of how much power to delegate to a central authority versus how much to retain in the states was irresolvable in the abstract and would require specific, material concessions by large states to small states.

**4. Legal Validation of Wartime Governance**  
Until the Articles took effect in 1781, the Continental Congress governed the war and the colonies without a legal constitution—it was a de facto authority claiming powers it had not been granted by any document. The 1778 ratifications moved the Congress from de facto toward de jure authority (even if still incomplete until Maryland ratified in 1781). This mattered both domestically and internationally. It gave Congress's acts—its requisitions for supplies, its appointment of officers, its coordination of military campaigns—a constitutional basis that bolstered their moral and legal force. It also mattered to France and other foreign powers contemplating recognition and aid: a union with a visible constitutional framework and demonstrable state ratification appeared more stable and reliable than one without. The 1778 ratifications thus served as an instrument of foreign policy as well as domestic governance.

## Background and Context

**The Need for Constitutional Framework**  
When the Declaration of Independence was adopted on July 4, 1776, the thirteen colonies declared themselves "free and independent states," but they lacked any formal legal mechanism to coordinate their war effort. The Continental Congress, which had been convened since 1774 as a voluntary assembly of colonial delegates, governed by consensus and persuasion rather than constitutional authority. By 1777, as the war dragged on, it became clear that a more formal structure was needed—something that could organize the requisitioning of supplies, coordinate military campaigns, raise money, negotiate treaties, and settle disputes between states over boundary lines and war contributions. Congress drafted the Articles of Confederation to fill this gap.

**The Articles as a Statement of Principle**  
The Articles did more than create a structure for governance; they embodied a political principle: that a union could exist in which the states retained their sovereignty and independence. This was radical for its time. The eighteenth-century world knew empires (France, Spain, Britain) and kingdoms, but not federations in which multiple sovereign entities pooled some powers while retaining others. The Articles represented an unprecedented experiment in federalism, driven by the colonists' determination to avoid repeating the British experience of imperial centralization.

**Congress Approves and Submits**  
Congress approved the Articles on November 15, 1777, and began submitting them to the states for ratification. Ratification required the assent of all thirteen states—a unanimity requirement that made the process acutely vulnerable to any single state's objections. The Articles explicitly stated that they would not take effect until ratified by all states.

## The 1778 Ratification Surge

**February and March 1778: Ten States Ratify**  
By July 1778, ten of thirteen states had ratified the Articles. The timeline was:

- **South Carolina** — February 5, 1778
- **New York** — February 6, 1778 (the same day as the Franco-American alliance)
- **Rhode Island** — February 9, 1778
- **Connecticut** — February 12, 1778
- **Georgia** — February 26, 1778
- **New Hampshire** — March 4, 1778
- **Pennsylvania** — March 5, 1778
- **Massachusetts** — March 10, 1778
- **North Carolina** — April 5, 1778
- **New Jersey** — November 19, 1778

The rapidity of ratification in February and March 1778 reflected the transformative impact of the Franco-American alliance. The announcement of French support electrified American political leadership and public opinion alike. Ratification of the Articles became a demonstration of American unity and commitment—something France would have wanted to see before deploying its fleet. Each state's legislators viewed ratification as both a patriotic duty and a practical necessity: a formal union was essential to winning the war with French help, and France needed assurance that the American states were genuinely united before it committed its forces.

**The Engrossed Articles Signed (July 1778)**  
On June 26, 1778, Congress ordered the Articles engrossed on parchment, and on July 9, 1778, the delegates of eight of the ratifying states signed the engrossed document; delegates from Georgia and North Carolina added their signatures later in July as they became available. The signing formalized the commitment of the ratifying states, but the Articles would not take legal effect until all thirteen had ratified. New Jersey ratified on November 19, 1778 (its delegates signing on November 26), bringing the count to ten and leaving only Delaware and Maryland as holdouts by year's end.

## The Holdout States and the Western Lands Crisis (1778–1781)

**Delaware and Maryland Refuse**  
Two small states refused to ratify by year's end:

- **Delaware** — feared domination by larger neighbors and lacked western territorial claims that might give it an independent economic base. Delaware would ultimately ratify on February 1, 1779 (its delegate signing the engrossed Articles on February 22, 1779), after Congress gave assurances that the interests of small states would be protected under the union.
- **Maryland** — the most consequential holdout. Small and land-poor, Maryland identified a decisive leverage point: it refused ratification until Virginia and other land-claiming states ceded their western claims to the federal government. Maryland's legislature passed a resolution explicitly conditioning ratification on this land cession, transforming a grievance into a constitutional demand that forced a genuine negotiation over the nature of the federal union.

Maryland's position, while seemingly obstructionist, reflected a genuine constitutional concern: if Virginia, New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and North Carolina each retained vast territories west of the Appalachian Mountains, those states could grow exponentially in wealth and power while states like Maryland, New Jersey, and Delaware remained confined to their original boundaries. Over time, the land-poor states would be dominated by land-rich ones within the union.

**The Western Lands Dispute (1778–1780)**  
The dispute over western lands raged throughout 1778 and 1779. Maryland remained adamant: it would not ratify the Articles until Virginia and the other land-claiming states surrendered their western claims to the union. The dispute threatened to unravel the entire federal project. A union that could not achieve unanimity had no legitimacy; yet three states held the power to block ratification indefinitely.

**Resolution (1779–1781)**  
The deadlock broke in stages. Delaware ratified on February 1, 1779. Virginia's General Assembly then passed a resolution on January 2, 1781, agreeing to cede its enormous northwestern territory (what would become Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, and Wisconsin) to the federal union—a concession of immense political and economic significance. Virginia's land cession removed Maryland's only stated objection. Maryland's legislature ratified on February 2, 1781, and its delegates signed the engrossed Articles on March 1, 1781, the day Congress declared the Articles in effect—nearly four years after Congressional approval and three years after the 1778 ratification surge. The union formed by ten states in 1778 was finally made whole.

## The British Response: Carlisle Commission and the Articles' Significance

The Articles' 1778 ratification surge did not go unnoticed in London. The British government, alarmed by the Franco-American alliance signed February 6, 1778, dispatched the Carlisle Peace Commission to Philadelphia in late spring 1778 to offer the Americans substantial concessions—including virtual self-governance—in exchange for renouncing independence. The Commission arrived in June 1778, just as ratifications were accelerating, and found itself dealing with a Congress that—emboldened by the ratification surge and the French alliance—was determined to reject any settlement that did not acknowledge full independence. The Commission's instructions authorized concessions to a "Congress," implicitly recognizing that body as the legitimate American interlocutor. Congress, emboldened by the Articles and by the French alliance, rejected all British overtures.

The Carlisle Commission's failure demonstrated that the Articles served as a constitutional shield against negotiated settlement: by giving Congress a legal basis for its actions and the states a shared framework, the ratification surge made it politically impossible for any single state to defect from the union and negotiate a separate peace. Maryland's holdout had not broken the union's practical functioning; the ten-state ratification had provided all the constitutional authority Congress needed to conduct the war.

---

## Significance of 1778 in Retrospect

While final ratification did not occur until 1781, 1778 marked the critical watershed: the ten-state ratification demonstrated a clear majority consensus, authorized Congress to act as the de facto federal government, and provided France with the assurance it needed that the American cause was a genuine union rather than a temporary military coalition. The 1778 ratifications transformed the Revolutionary War from a colonial rebellion into a federal war of independence, fought by the thirteen united states rather than by Massachusetts or Virginia alone.

The dispute over western lands, brought to a head by Maryland's holdout, established a pattern that would recur: the tension between state sovereignty and federal union would be resolved through compromise and material concession, not through abstract principle. This pattern would intensify in 1787, when the Constitutional Convention would spend three months debating the same fundamental issues—state sovereignty versus federal power, representation based on population or equal for all states, and the question of western territories—and would produce a second federal constitution to replace the inadequate Articles.
