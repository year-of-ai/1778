# Voltaire's Irène

**Category**: Arts & Culture  
**Key figures**: Voltaire (François-Marie Arouet), Marie Antoinette, Comédie-Française, Marie-Françoise Dumesnil (actress), Jean-Baptiste-Antoine Suard (critic)

## Summary

*Irène*, a tragedy in five acts, premiered on March 16, 1778 at the Comédie-Française's Théâtre des Tuileries in Paris, written by Voltaire (1694–1778) in his final year. The play is set in the Byzantine Empire during the reign of Constantine VII and centers on the heroine Irène, a noble Greek woman caught in a triangle of romantic and political intrigue involving the Byzantine emperor and a younger warrior prince. The play adheres to the classical tragic conventions Voltaire had championed throughout his career — the three unities of time, place, and action; elevated verse; and the portrayal of virtuous protagonists overcome by fate and circumstance. The premiere took place before an audience that included Marie Antoinette; Voltaire, at age 83 and in failing health, was too ill to attend but received dispatches after each act reporting the audience's reception.

The play achieved considerable success, in large part as a tribute to Voltaire's legendary status and his triumphant return to Paris after 28 years in exile at his estate Ferney. At the sixth performance on March 30, Voltaire attended his triumph in person despite his advanced age and infirmity. During this performance, his bust was crowned on stage by members of the Comédie-Française — a ceremonial honor that became a symbol of his apotheosis in French culture. The crowning proved to be one of his final public appearances; he died two months later on May 30, 1778. The play was published in multiple editions following its success and became the capstone of Voltaire's long dramatic career, which had begun with *Œdipe* in 1718.

## Background: Voltaire's Dramatic Career (1718–1778)

### Sixty Years of Tragedy

When *Irène* premiered, Voltaire had been writing for the stage for sixty years — longer than virtually any other major European playwright. His first tragedy, *Œdipe* (1718), had won him fame at age twenty-four and established his identity as the preeminent French tragedian of his generation. Over the following decades he produced at least fifty plays, the most celebrated including *Zaïre* (1732), *Alzire* (1736), *Mahomet* (1741), *Mérope* (1743), and *Tancrède* (1760). His dramatic output rivaled his philosophical writing in volume and for much of his career exceeded it in public prestige.

Voltaire championed the classical French tragic tradition of Corneille and Racine, insisting on strict adherence to the three unities (time, place, action) and on the edifying function of tragedy — the representation of virtue tested by fate. This classicist allegiance placed him in implicit opposition to the emerging sensibilities of the later 18th century: the "comédie larmoyante" (tearful comedy), the bourgeois drama championed by Diderot, and the Gothic and sentimental currents that would shape the Romantic movement. His final tragedy *Irène* thus stands as a defense, in the year of his death, of the form he had spent his life perfecting.

### Classical Form Under Pressure

By 1778, the classical tragedy's cultural authority was under challenge. The operatic reformer Christoph Willibald Gluck had recently transformed the Opéra with works that prioritized dramatic intensity over formal convention — the [Gluck–Piccinni Querelle](gluck-piccinni-querelle-1778.md) raging in Paris at precisely the moment of *Irène*'s premiere. The bourgeois drama had opened the stage to domestic settings and middle-class protagonists. Diderot's theories of drama advocated natural dialogue and realistic representation, which Voltaire's stiff alexandrines and classical subject matter deliberately refused. *Irène* in this context was as much a manifesto as a play: Voltaire's insistence on classical form in the final year of his life was a statement that the tradition he had championed retained its validity.

## The Play: Plot and Classical Conventions

*Irène* unfolds in a single day (the unity of time) in the Byzantine court (the unity of place). The heroine Irène is contracted to marry the elderly Emperor Constantine, whom she does not love; she is in love with Alexis, a younger military hero who has distinguished himself in the empire's service. The plot turns on a series of confrontations — between Irène and the emperor, between Alexis and the court faction opposing him, and between public duty and private feeling — that escalate toward a catastrophic resolution. Irène's choice, driven by honor and political necessity, leads ultimately to her death; the tragedy ends with the emperor's regret and Alexis's grief.

The verse is in the classical alexandrine couplet (twelve-syllable rhyming lines in French), the formal medium of French tragedy since Corneille. Contemporaries and subsequent critics alike noted that the verse showed the fatigue of composition — Voltaire was 83 and writing in failing health — but also retained moments of genuine rhetorical power. The play's flaws were forgiven by audiences and critics who received it as a tribute to its author rather than a purely disinterested aesthetic judgment.

## The Return to Paris and the Six Performances

### Voltaire's Triumphant Return

Voltaire arrived in Paris on February 5, 1778, after 28 years of effective exile. He had last lived in the city in 1750, and his return — by then 83 years old, his health fragile — was a cultural event without modern parallel. Crowds gathered outside the Hôtel de Villette, where he lodged with his niece's family, to catch a glimpse of him. He was visited by the leading figures of Parisian intellectual and artistic life: d'Alembert, the Marquis de Condorcet, Turgot, and many others.

His visit to the Comédie-Française for a rehearsal of *Irène* — the first time many of the younger Parisian actors had seen him — provoked a scene of near-hysteria. The actors reportedly wept. The public attending rehearsals demanded to see him, and he was forced to appear in the box to acknowledge applause. This atmosphere of collective homage shaped the premiere itself.

### The Premiere: March 16, 1778

The premiere of *Irène* on March 16 was attended by Marie Antoinette and members of the royal family — an unusual honor for a playwright who had spent his career in conflict with royal authority, though by 1778 Voltaire's rehabilitation was essentially complete. The audience responded with sustained enthusiasm. Reports circulated that the enthusiasm was partly theatrical loyalty to a legendary name rather than simple aesthetic response, but contemporaries who recorded the evening — including the critic Jean-Baptiste-Antoine Suard — noted genuine excitement in the house.

Voltaire received dispatches from the theater after each act, waiting anxiously at his lodgings for news of the reception. The overall report was favorable, and the play ran to six full performances.

### The Crowning: March 30, 1778

The sixth performance, on March 30, 1778, marked the climax of Voltaire's apotheosis in Paris. He attended in person — an achievement given his extreme age and failing health — and took his seat in a box visible to the audience. At a signal during the performance, members of the Comédie-Française brought his bust onto the stage and crowned it with a laurel wreath in a formal ceremony. This act of theatrical apotheosis — a ritual borrowed from ancient Roman triumph — was wholly unprecedented in French theatrical culture. Twelve actors participated in the crowning ceremony; the crowd rose in sustained applause.

The scene was witnessed by hundreds and described in letters and journals across Europe within days. Voltaire, overwhelmed, reportedly wept. The event was immediately understood as a cultural marker: the theatrical coronation of a living writer as the personification of French intellectual civilization. Voltaire died two months later on May 30, making this performance one of his last public appearances.

## Connection to the Franco-American Alliance

*Irène*'s premiere on March 16, 1778 fell one day before the British government's formal declaration that it considered itself at war with France — a consequence of the [Treaty of Alliance](../politics/franco-american-alliance.md) France had signed with the American rebels on February 6. Paris in March 1778 was simultaneously celebrating Voltaire's theatrical triumph and bracing for a major European war. Benjamin Franklin, whose diplomatic success had secured the alliance just weeks before, was still in the city; he would meet Voltaire at the Académie des sciences on April 29, 1778, in one of the most celebrated encounters of the age.

## Significance

*Irène* represents the culmination of Voltaire's 60-year career as a dramatist and the symbolic closing of the Enlightenment's dramatic tradition. The play's premiere and his crowning on stage mark the climactic moment of Voltaire's literary apotheosis in Paris — a recognition not of the play's artistic merit (later critics found it among his weaker tragedies) but of his cultural titanism. The 1778 premiere is inseparable from the broader 1778 cultural moment: Voltaire's return and triumph occurred weeks before Jean-Jacques Rousseau's death (July 2, 1778), and together the two philosophers' exits from the stage mark the passing of the Enlightenment's founding generation.

### The Last Classical Tragedy

As the final work of the age's most celebrated tragedian, *Irène* is also an artifact of classicism's twilight. Voltaire's insistence on the three unities and the alexandrine form in 1778 stood in deliberate opposition to the dramatic reforms of Diderot and Gluck and foreshadowed the Romantic revolution that would overturn them within two decades. Victor Hugo's famous "Preface to Cromwell" (1827), which explicitly attacked the three unities and demanded a new naturalistic drama, was written in implicit dialogue with precisely the tradition Voltaire's final play exemplified. In this sense, *Irène* closed an era — the era of French neoclassical drama — that would not reopen.

### Cross-Arts Context: 1778's Literary Moment

The premiere of *Irène* in March 1778 belongs to a year of remarkable cultural productivity across European capitals. In the same year, Fanny Burney published [*Evelina*](evelina-burney.md) in London (January 1778) — a debut novel that likewise established the domestic social novel as a serious form; [La Scala opened in Milan](la-scala.md) (August 3); and [Mozart premiered his "Paris" Symphony](mozart-paris-symphony.md) at the Concert Spirituel (June 18). Together these events mark 1778 as a year of cultural transition: the old modes of aristocratic patronage and classical form giving way to new institutions, new voices, and new aesthetic assumptions.
