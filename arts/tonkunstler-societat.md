# Tonkünstler-Societät (Vienna's Concert Society)

**Category**: Arts & Culture  
**Key figures**: Florian Gassmann (founder), Empress Maria Theresa (imperial patron), Christoph Willibald Gluck, Franz Joseph Haydn

## Summary

The Tonkünstler-Societät (officially *Gesellschaft der Wiener Tonkünstler zum Unterhalte ihrer Witwen und Waisen* — "Society of Viennese Musicians for the Support of their Widows and Orphans") was established in 1771 with an imperial decree from Empress Maria Theresa and formal authorization dated February 23, 1771. Founded by composer and conductor Florian Gassmann, the society was created to provide financial support and social security for retired musicians and their families — addressing a critical gap in Viennese musical community welfare. Maria Theresa made an initial contribution of 500 ducats (about 2,000 florins) to the society's fund, and members supported the endowment through enrollment fees and dues.

Beginning in 1772, the Tonkünstler-Societät organized regular benefit concerts to generate revenue for its charitable mission. By 1778 — the period of its consolidation as Vienna's premier concert institution — the society held two concert series annually, typically during Easter and before Christmas, primarily at Vienna's Burgtheater. This concert schedule was remarkable for its era: prior to the Tonkünstler-Societät, public concerts in Vienna were rare and sporadic, with music-making confined largely to the imperial court and religious institutions.

## Founding and Early Structure

Florian Gassmann (1729–1774) served as Kapellmeister at the Vienna court when he petitioned Maria Theresa to charter the society. His vision was explicitly welfare-oriented: musicians faced old age without pensions, and their survivors had no safety net after a performer's death. The society drew its membership from the professional musicians of Vienna's theaters and the imperial court, building a broad base from the outset.

Gassmann died in January 1774, just three years after founding the organization. His successor as chief organizer was court composer Giuseppe Bonno, who guided the society through the mid-1770s. The society's governing board included both musicians and aristocratic patrons, an early example of the hybrid civil-society institutions that would characterize Viennese cultural life throughout the classical era.

By 1778 the society had become the established pension fund for Vienna's professional musicians, sustained by members' enrollment fees and dues alongside the proceeds of its benefit concerts, and able to make regular payouts to the widows and orphans of deceased members.

## Concert Program and Featured Works

The society's benefit concerts were not merely charity fundraisers but events of genuine artistic prestige. The Easter and Advent series at the Burgtheater regularly drew Vienna's educated public, and the programs featured large-scale choral and orchestral works that individual aristocrats could not easily mount at private academies.

The Tonkünstler-Societät was the primary Viennese venue for oratorio performance in the 1770s and 1780s. Works by Gassmann, Carl Ditters von Dittersdorf, and visiting Italian composers featured regularly. In later decades the society would mount the premieres of some of Haydn's greatest choral works: *Il ritorno di Tobia* (1775 and 1784) and, most famously, *The Creation* (*Die Schöpfung*) in April and May 1798. The 1798 *Creation* performances, under Haydn's personal direction at the Schwarzenberg Palace and then the Burgtheater, drew the largest concert audiences Vienna had seen to that date.

Concert admission was set at a rate accessible to the middle classes, which distinguished the society's events from exclusive court concerts. Ticket revenues could be considerable: the 1785 premiere of Mozart's cantata *Davide penitente*, for example, took in 733 florins on its opening night, with such proceeds underwriting the society's payments to dependent families.

## Haydn and the Tonkünstler-Societät

Franz Joseph Haydn's relationship with the society illustrates both the organization's influence and its tensions with leading composers. In 1778 Haydn applied for membership and paid his enrollment fee, but the society then required that he compose further works for it free of charge on demand. Haydn refused these terms, and the society rejected his membership. Relations remained cold for years before a compromise was reached for the spring 1784 performances of his oratorio *Il ritorno di Tobia*. Haydn did not finally join the society until 1797, when it made him an honorary member in recognition of his many contributions to its charitable concerts.

Despite the early estrangement, Haydn collaborated with the society when it served artistic goals. His oratorio *Il ritorno di Tobia* — first performed under the society's auspices in 1775 — was one of the major Viennese premieres of the era, and the 1798 *Creation* performances under the society's umbrella cemented his reputation as the greatest living composer. The episode illustrates a recurring tension in 18th-century music: the society held institutional power over Vienna's concert life, yet individual composers of stature could resist its terms without professional ruin, so long as they retained court patronage (as Haydn did through Prince Esterházy).

## Legacy

Until the founding of the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde in 1811, the Tonkünstler-Societät remained the only private organization in Vienna capable of organizing regular public concerts. This forty-year monopoly on institutional concert-giving made the society extraordinarily influential in determining which works reached the public ear and which composers gained prominence.

The society's model proved influential across Europe. Similar charitable musicians' societies modeled on Vienna's example were established in Berlin (1801) and St. Petersburg (1802), spreading the institutional framework for public concert life. The Tonkünstler-Societät thus represents a pivotal moment in the professionalization and democratization of classical music: music moved from exclusive court and ecclesiastical settings into public benefit performances accessible to paying audiences.

The society's significance in 1778 specifically — when it was consolidating its position as Vienna's musical institution — parallels the opening of [La Scala in Milan](./la-scala.md) on August 3, 1778. Both events represent the broader European trajectory of the 1770s toward institutional, public, and commercial music-making. Where La Scala epitomized operatic grandeur and aristocratic culture, the Tonkünstler-Societät represented music's expansion into the middle-class public sphere through charitable benefit concerts.

## Significance

The Tonkünstler-Societät pioneered institutional concert culture in Vienna and, by extension, across Central Europe. Its charity mandate gave it a social legitimacy that purely commercial concerts lacked in the 18th century, allowing it to grow under imperial protection while simultaneously opening music to a broader public. The society survived well into the 19th century under the name *Tonkünstler-Orchester*, eventually evolving into a professional symphony orchestra that still performs in Vienna today.

The 1778 moment — when the society was fully operational and attracting major talent while the Viennese classical style was reaching its first maturity under Haydn and the young Mozart — makes this institution central to any account of how the great classical repertoire found its audience. Without the Tonkünstler-Societät's concert infrastructure, the public reception of late 18th-century Viennese music would have been far more confined to court and church.
