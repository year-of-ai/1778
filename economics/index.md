# Economics & Trade — 1778

Economic conditions and commercial developments of 1778.

The year 1778 witnessed an economic rupture for the American colonies and their British adversary. The Franco-American alliance and Britain's naval blockade fundamentally reoriented colonial commerce away from British markets toward France, the Caribbean, and privateering ventures. This forced redirection created both crisis and opportunity: established trade networks collapsed, yet new commercial pathways opened through French and Caribbean ports. Simultaneously, the Continental dollar faced severe inflationary pressure—by year's end it had plummeted to one-fifth or one-seventh of its face value due to overprinting, counterfeiting, and the absence of coordinated fiscal policy. This financial crisis forced the young American nation into dependence on French loans and credit, effectively mortgaging its independence to French financial backing. The economic year of 1778 thus encapsulated the war's central dilemma: military victory required external support, and external support carried the price of debt and vulnerability that would haunt the new nation for years to come. Across the Atlantic, the same year advanced Britain's early Industrial Revolution—Boulton & Watt's fuel-efficient steam engines, Arkwright's spreading water-frame mills, and the casting of the world's first iron bridge at Coalbrookdale—underscoring the widening productive gap between the industrializing British war economy and the craft-based American one. A dedicated account of the war's financing traces how French loans, privateering profits, and the private credit of financiers such as Robert Morris sustained the Continental war effort when public revenue failed.
