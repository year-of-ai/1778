# George Rogers Clark's Illinois Campaign (1778)

**Category**: Military Events  
**Key figures**: George Rogers Clark, Patrick Henry (Governor of Virginia), Father Pierre Gibault, Lieutenant Governor Henry Hamilton (British), Captain Joseph Bowman, Captain Leonard Helm

## Summary

The Illinois Campaign of 1778 was a Virginia militia expedition into the British-held Illinois Country — the region northwest of the Ohio River in present-day Illinois and Indiana — led by 25-year-old Lieutenant Colonel George Rogers Clark. Authorized secretly by Virginia Governor Patrick Henry, Clark led approximately 175 men down the Ohio River and overland to capture the British post of Kaskaskia on the night of July 4, 1778, without firing a shot. Within days, the nearby settlements of Cahokia and Prairie du Rocher also came under American control, and by August the inhabitants of Vincennes on the Wabash River had accepted American authority.

The campaign opened the western theater of the Revolutionary War and established American presence in a vast territory previously controlled by Britain. Virginia organized the captured territory as Illinois County in December 1778. Although the British retook Vincennes in December 1778 under Lieutenant Governor Henry Hamilton — and Clark's subsequent recapture of it at Fort Sackville in February 1779 completed the campaign — the 1778 Illinois operations laid the groundwork for American territorial claims to the trans-Appalachian west that shaped the 1783 Treaty of Paris.

## Background: The Illinois Country and British Control

The Illinois Country was a vast region between the Ohio River and the Great Lakes, encompassing the Mississippi River valley settlements of Kaskaskia, Cahokia, and Vincennes. France had held the region until 1763, when the Treaty of Paris ending the Seven Years' War transferred it to Britain. By 1778, the area held fewer than 1,000 European inhabitants — primarily French-speaking descendants of earlier settlers — plus approximately 600 enslaved African Americans. British military presence was minimal; regulars had largely withdrawn by 1775, leaving only small garrisons and the influence of Lieutenant Governor Henry Hamilton at Detroit to maintain British authority and coordinate Indigenous alliances.

Indigenous peoples — including Shawnee, Miami, Potawatomi, Odawa, and Ojibwe nations — inhabited or traversed the region in large numbers. Hamilton, known to American frontiersmen as "Hair-Buyer" for his alleged payment for American scalps (a charge disputed by some historians), coordinated British-allied raiding parties against American settlements in Kentucky and western Virginia. These raids on the Kentucky frontier were the direct motivation for Clark's expedition.

## Planning and Authorization

George Rogers Clark, born November 19, 1752, in Albemarle County, Virginia, had been a surveyor and land speculator in Kentucky before the war. By 1777, he had concluded that the attacks on Kentucky settlements could only be stopped by eliminating their British source in the Illinois Country. In December 1777, he presented his plan to Governor Patrick Henry, arguing that a small force could seize the lightly garrisoned British posts and win over the French-speaking inhabitants sympathetic to the American cause.

Henry authorized the expedition in January 1778 — secretly, to prevent British forewarning — with a public cover story that the soldiers were being recruited for Kentucky defense. Clark received a commission as lieutenant colonel and authorization to recruit up to 350 men, organized into companies. In practice, recruitment difficulties and desertion meant only about 175 men would make the final march on Kaskaskia.

Clark also carried a second, secret set of orders: if he found the French inhabitants amenable to the American cause, he was to treat with them on terms favorable to Virginia's claim to the territory.

## The Expedition

### Descent of the Ohio (May–June 1778)

Clark departed Redstone, Pennsylvania (present-day Brownsville) on May 12–13, 1778, moving down the Monongahela and Ohio rivers by flatboat. He established a training base on Corn Island near the Falls of the Ohio (present-day Louisville, Kentucky), where he drilled his men and received news — likely from dispatches — of the [Franco-American Alliance](../politics/franco-american-alliance.md) concluded in February. The alliance news would prove strategically valuable: French-speaking inhabitants of the Illinois Country would be more likely to accept American authority once they learned France and America were allies.

The men passed Fort Massac (an abandoned French post on the Ohio River in present-day Illinois) and concealed their boats. Clark chose an overland route from the Ohio River to Kaskaskia to avoid detection from river traffic.

### Capture of Kaskaskia (July 4, 1778)

Clark's force of approximately 175 men marched overland roughly 120 miles in six days, moving at night and avoiding main routes. They arrived at the Kaskaskia River hungry and exhausted on the evening of July 4, 1778. Local settlers were captured to prevent warning and compelled to guide the force across the river.

Clark's men entered Kaskaskia at midnight on July 4–5, securing the village without firing a single shot. The British post's commander, Philippe de Rocheblave, was captured asleep in his quarters. Clark's force took control of the settlement and its small fort without resistance.

The date — July 4, the second anniversary of the Declaration of Independence — was coincidental but became part of the campaign's mythology. Clark used the news of the French alliance immediately: he informed the frightened French inhabitants that France and America were now allies, and that they could expect to be treated as friends rather than conquered enemies if they swore loyalty to Virginia and the United States. Father Pierre Gibault, the local Catholic priest, became a crucial intermediary, helping persuade the community to accept American authority.

### Expansion to Cahokia and Vincennes

Within two days of Kaskaskia's capture, Captain Joseph Bowman led a company northward along the Mississippi River and secured Cahokia and Prairie du Rocher — also without armed resistance. The French inhabitants at these settlements, like those at Kaskaskia, were persuaded through a combination of the French alliance news and Gibault's mediation to swear loyalty to Virginia.

In mid-July, Father Gibault volunteered to travel to Vincennes on the Wabash River, approximately 180 miles to the east, to bring the news of the French alliance and persuade that community to join the American cause. Gibault succeeded: the inhabitants of Vincennes accepted American authority and raised the American flag. Captain Leonard Helm was sent with a small detachment to take formal command of Fort Sackville at Vincennes.

### Virginia Establishes Illinois County

The political consolidation of Clark's gains followed rapidly. Virginia, which claimed the Illinois Country under its colonial charter, organized the captured territory as **Illinois County** in December 1778, with Kaskaskia as the county seat. Clark was named commander of the county's military forces.

## British Counteroffensive and the 1779 Aftermath

The British response came swiftly. Lieutenant Governor Henry Hamilton at Detroit learned of Clark's captures in August 1778 and organized a counteroffensive. Leading a force of Detroit Volunteers, militia, artillery, and Indigenous warriors drawn from Odawa, Ojibwe, Miami, Potawatomi, and Shawnee nations — totaling several hundred men — Hamilton moved south through the autumn. He recaptured Vincennes and Fort Sackville on December 17, 1778, with Helm's tiny garrison surrendering after brief resistance.

The recapture of Vincennes threatened to unravel Clark's gains. However, Clark's response in February 1779 — a celebrated winter march of 170 men across flooded Illinois terrain — resulted in the surprise siege and recapture of Fort Sackville on February 23–25, 1779. Hamilton surrendered 79 men and was sent as a prisoner to Virginia. This second capture of Vincennes completed the military phase of the campaign and restored American control of the Illinois Country, though Clark's larger ambition of capturing Detroit was never fulfilled.

## Significance

### Opening the Western Theater

The Illinois Campaign of 1778 opened a western theater of the Revolutionary War that had previously been entirely under British control. Before Clark's expedition, the Ohio Valley and Illinois Country functioned as a base for British-coordinated raids against American settlements in Kentucky and western Virginia. The campaign temporarily disrupted this operational base and extended American military presence hundreds of miles beyond the established frontier.

### Virginia's Territorial Claims

By organizing Illinois County in December 1778, Virginia formalized its claim to a vast territory whose legal status was disputed even among the American states. When peace negotiations began in Paris in 1781–1782, American diplomats — including [Benjamin Franklin](../people/benjamin-franklin.md) — advanced claims to the trans-Appalachian west based in part on effective occupation and governance. The Illinois Campaign was a component of the evidence for those claims.

### The 1783 Treaty of Paris

The Treaty of Paris (1783) extended the southern boundary of the United States to the 31st parallel and the western boundary to the Mississippi River — granting the new nation control of the Northwest Territory, including the former Illinois Country. Historians debate how much Clark's campaign influenced this outcome; some credit it as decisive, while others, notably historian Lowell Harrison, argue the campaign amounted to temporary occupation rather than conquest and that its influence on the peace terms has been overstated. What is undeniable is that American occupation and governance of the Illinois Country during 1778–1783 provided a factual basis for American negotiators to cite.

### Connection to the Franco-American Alliance

The French alliance negotiated by [Benjamin Franklin](../people/benjamin-franklin.md) and signed in February 1778 proved operationally important to Clark's campaign. The announcement of the alliance at Kaskaskia and Vincennes was the primary instrument by which Clark converted the French-speaking population from potential adversaries to active supporters. Without the alliance, Father Gibault's mediation and the bloodless surrender of the Illinois settlements might not have been possible.
