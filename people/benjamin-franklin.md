# Benjamin Franklin

**Category**: Key Figures
**Key figures**: Benjamin Franklin, Louis XVI, Comte de Vergennes (French Foreign Minister), Silas Deane, John Adams, Conrad Alexandre Gérard (first French minister to the United States)

## Summary

Benjamin Franklin (January 17, 1706 – April 17, 1790) was an American polymath, inventor, diplomat, and one of the founding fathers of the United States. In 1778, at age 72, he was the dominant American diplomatic presence in Europe, residing at the **Hôtel de Valentinois** in Passy on the outskirts of Paris. His mission — to secure French military and financial support for the American Revolution — reached its culmination on **February 6, 1778**, when France and the United States signed the **Treaty of Alliance** and the **Treaty of Amity and Commerce**, the first foreign military alliance in American history.

Franklin had arrived in France in **December 1776** as a member of the American diplomatic commission alongside Silas Deane and Arthur Lee. The turning point came with the American victory at **Saratoga** (October 17, 1777), which convinced Vergennes and Louis XVI that the Continental Army could sustain a viable war effort. Within weeks, France moved from covert assistance to formal alliance. On **March 20, 1778**, Franklin and his colleagues were presented at Versailles to Louis XVI — the first time an American diplomat was received at the French court. In **September 1778**, Franklin was appointed **Minister Plenipotentiary** to France, making him the first American to hold that rank with a foreign government.

Franklin remained in France until **July 1785**, nine years in all, longer than any other American founder spent abroad. During that time he negotiated the loans that kept the Continental Congress solvent, coordinated the dispatch of French troops and ships, and shaped the negotiations that produced the **Treaty of Paris** (1783), ending the Revolutionary War.

## Arrival and Early Diplomacy (1776–1777)

Franklin sailed from Philadelphia in October 1776 and arrived at Auray, Brittany, on December 3, 1776, after a rough six-week Atlantic crossing. He was already 70 years old and in indifferent health, suffering from gout and kidney stones that would plague him throughout his French years. Despite this, he plunged immediately into diplomacy, reaching Paris in late December and establishing himself at Passy.

France under Louis XVI was providing covert aid through the fictional trading company **Rodrigue Hortalez et Cie**, organized by dramatist-turned-spymaster Pierre-Augustin de Beaumarchais, channeling gunpowder, cannon, and supplies to the Continental Army. Franklin's role was to convert this shadow support into an open alliance. His obstacle was Vergennes's caution: France needed to be certain the Americans would not negotiate a separate peace with Britain before France had extracted strategic gains.

## The Saratoga Effect and the Treaties of February 1778

News of the American victory at **Saratoga** (the surrender of General Burgoyne's army of approximately 5,800 men on October 17, 1777) reached Versailles in early December 1777 and transformed the diplomatic calculus. Vergennes recognized that Britain, now facing the prospect of a protracted war, might offer Americans reconciliation terms that could end the rebellion without French involvement. He moved quickly.

Negotiations concluded in January 1778. The two treaties signed on **February 6, 1778** at the Hôtel de Lautrec in Paris committed both parties:

- **Treaty of Alliance**: France pledged to fight alongside the United States until American independence was "formally or tacitly assured." Neither party could conclude peace with Britain without the other's consent.
- **Treaty of Amity and Commerce**: Established most-favored-nation trading relations and granted France commercial access to American ports.

The treaties were kept secret briefly; Britain learned of them in mid-March 1778 and immediately recalled its ambassador from Paris, effectively declaring a state of belligerency that would become formal war later in the year. See [Britain Declares War on France](../politics/britain-declares-war-france.md).

## Life in Passy and Social Influence

Franklin's residence at the Hôtel de Valentinois, provided rent-free by the wealthy merchant Jacques Donatien Le Ray de Chaumont, became the operational center of American diplomacy in Europe. Franklin operated a small **printing press** at Passy, producing French-language pamphlets, bagatelles (witty short essays), and diplomatic correspondence. The press allowed him to shape his own public image with a care unusual for the era.

His public persona was a studied construction. Franklin wore plain American clothing and a **beaver-fur cap** rather than the powdered wigs and embroidered coats expected at Versailles. French society read this as republican authenticity and Enlightenment simplicity — a philosopher-statesman from the New World uncorrupted by Old World luxury. Portraits and medallions bearing his image circulated throughout France; Madame du Barry, Louis XV's former mistress, reportedly wore his likeness as a brooch.

On **April 29, 1778**, Franklin appeared at the **Académie des Sciences** alongside the aging **Voltaire** — the two most famous intellectuals of the Enlightenment sharing a stage. According to accounts, the audience demanded they embrace in the French manner; they kissed each other's cheeks to sustained applause. Voltaire died on May 30, 1778, less than five weeks later; Rousseau died on July 2, 1778. Franklin outlived them both but carried their intellectual legacy forward in his person. See [Voltaire](voltaire.md) and [Rousseau](rousseau.md).

## Scientific Reputation as Diplomatic Tool

Franklin's scientific fame — he had demonstrated lightning's electrical nature in the early 1750s and invented the lightning rod, bifocals, the Franklin stove, and the glass armonica — gave him a credibility in European intellectual circles that no other American diplomat could have matched. He had been a Fellow of the **Royal Society** since 1756, and French academies welcomed him as a peer.

This standing mattered diplomatically. French intellectuals who might have dismissed American independence as a colonial quarrel were predisposed to support a cause championed by a man who had traded correspondence with the great scientists of Europe for two decades. Franklin understood this and played the role deliberately, attending salons, corresponding with philosophes, and presenting the American Revolution as the political application of Enlightenment natural rights theory.

## Military and Financial Consequences

The immediate military effect of the alliance Franklin secured materialized quickly. A French fleet under **Comte d'Estaing** set sail from Toulon in April 1778, arriving off the American coast in July 1778 — the first major French naval intervention. Though d'Estaing's operations in 1778 (off Newport and Savannah) were inconclusive, the French naval commitment prevented Britain from achieving uncontested sea control, a shift with long-term strategic consequences.

Franklin's ongoing role in securing **French loans** proved equally critical. The Continental Congress was chronically insolvent; French lending — ultimately totaling tens of millions of livres — sustained American military operations through the war's middle years. The military climax came in 1781, when a combined Franco-American force besieged **Yorktown** while the French fleet under de Grasse blocked British naval relief — a campaign that ended effective British resistance and that would have been impossible without the foundation Franklin had built in Paris.

## Legacy

Franklin left France in July 1785, aged 79, escorted to his ship by an honor guard arranged by Louis XVI. His nine years in Paris had transformed the diplomatic standing of the United States and profoundly influenced French political culture. The ideas he had championed — republican self-governance, natural rights, institutional restraint of arbitrary power — circulated in the salons and pamphlets of pre-Revolutionary France. Historians have noted that the ideological fertilization of French political thought that Franklin and the American example provided contributed to the intellectual atmosphere in which the **French Revolution** (1789) became conceivable.

Franklin died on April 17, 1790, in Philadelphia, at age 84. The French National Assembly voted three days of mourning — an honor unprecedented for a foreign citizen.
