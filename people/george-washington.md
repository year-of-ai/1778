# George Washington (1732–1799)

**Category**: Key Figures  
**Key figures**: George Washington, Baron von Steuben, General Charles Lee, General Henry Clinton, Continental Congress

## Valley Forge: The Scale of the Crisis

George Washington, born February 22, 1732, in Westmoreland County, Virginia, entered the Valley Forge encampment (December 19, 1777 – June 19, 1778) commanding approximately 11,000 men — a force already diminished by the defeats at Brandywine (September 11, 1777) and Germantown (October 4, 1777) and the loss of Philadelphia to British General William Howe. The physical conditions at Valley Forge were catastrophic. Without adequate food, clothing, or shelter, some 2,000–2,500 soldiers died of disease — primarily typhus, typhoid fever, dysentery, and pneumonia — during the six-month encampment, representing roughly a 20–25 percent fatality rate from illness alone. Thousands more were unfit for duty at any given time. Washington's January 1778 reports to the Continental Congress documented that nearly 4,000 men were "unfit for duty from want of shoes" and that the army was receiving provisions in quantities sufficient for perhaps two or three days at a time, with complete interruptions in supply lasting a week or more.

Washington's personal presence at Valley Forge was calculated and consequential. He refused to take winter quarters at a comfortable private house as senior officers typically did, instead maintaining his command headquarters in a stone house (now preserved as the Washington Headquarters at Valley Forge National Historical Park) and making himself visibly present among the troops. His wife Martha Washington arrived in February 1778 and remained through June, organizing hospital care and demonstrating that the commander's family shared the army's privations. These symbolic acts of solidarity maintained troop morale and officer loyalty during months when mass desertion was a constant risk.

## The Conway Cabal: Political Threats to Washington's Command

While Washington's army suffered at Valley Forge, a faction within Congress and the Continental Army sought to replace him as Commander-in-Chief. The "Conway Cabal" — named for Irish-born French general Thomas Conway, though the degree of actual conspiracy among its participants remains disputed by historians — reflected genuine dissatisfaction among Congressional delegates who blamed Washington for the fall of Philadelphia. The faction's preferred alternative was General Horatio Gates, who had recently won the decisive victory at Saratoga (October 1777) with relatively limited personal involvement. Conway wrote a private letter to Gates in October 1777 containing disparaging remarks about Washington, which Washington learned of and brought to the attention of Congress.

Washington's response to the Cabal was masterful. Rather than demanding Congressional action, he allowed the affair to unfold while maintaining the loyalty of his senior officers and demonstrating through continued operational presence that he could not be replaced without catastrophic risk to the army's cohesion. By February 1778, Conway had been forced to issue a public apology, Gates had distanced himself from the intrigue, and the Cabal's members had lost credibility. The episode strengthened rather than weakened Washington's position by revealing the absence of a viable alternative and the depth of loyalty among officers who publicly declared their confidence in his command.

## Summary

George Washington, born February 22, 1732, in Westmoreland County, Virginia, was the Commander-in-Chief of the Continental Army from 1775 to 1783 — the central military figure of the American Revolutionary War and architect of American independence. By 1778, at age 46, Washington had endured three years of warfare and faced the crisis that defined his leadership: the winter encampment at Valley Forge from December 1777 through June 1778, where his army faced starvation, disease, and demoralization while political enemies in Congress plotted his replacement.

The year 1778 marked Washington's transformation from a commander struggling to keep an army in the field into a general whose forces could stand against British regulars in open combat. When Baron Friedrich von Steuben arrived at Valley Forge in February 1778, Washington seized the opportunity to rebuild his army's discipline and training. By June, when the Continental Army departed Valley Forge and intercepted the British retreat from Philadelphia at the Battle of Monmouth, Washington's reinvigorated forces demonstrated the fruits of Steuben's training — holding their ground under fire, executing coordinated volleys, and forcing the British to withdraw. The battle vindicated Washington's strategy and secured his position as indispensable to the American cause, while also proving to foreign observers, particularly the French, that American forces were worthy partners in the alliance signed in February 1778.

Washington's leadership at Valley Forge and Monmouth also revealed his political acumen. When Major General Charles Lee, the second-in-command, ordered a retreat at Monmouth, Washington personally rallied the troops and assumed direct command — witnesses reported Washington's fury at finding Lee retreating without orders. Lee's subsequent court-martial, which began June 30 and concluded August 12, 1778, found him guilty of disobedience, misbehavior before the enemy, and disrespect to the Commander-in-Chief; he was suspended from command for a year and never reinstated. Lee's removal eliminated the greatest internal threat to Washington's authority. By the end of 1778, Washington had consolidated his position as both military commander and civilian political figure, answerable only to a distant Congress while maintaining the loyalty of his officers and the respect of his troops.

## Significance

Washington's 1778 represents a pivotal moment in the Revolutionary War. The survival of the Continental Army through Valley Forge, the training revolution under Steuben, and the successful engagement at Monmouth transformed American military capability and bolstered the Franco-American alliance. Without Washington's steady leadership during the encampment's hardships and his decisive action at Monmouth, the revolutionary cause might have collapsed. Washington's conduct in 1778 — his shared sacrifice (he endured the same privations as his troops, forgoing his comfortable Mount Vernon estate), his willingness to adapt (embracing Steuben's European methods), and his political skill (neutralizing internal rivals) — established him as the figure most essential to American independence.

Washington's victory at Monmouth also marked a turning point in British strategy. Britain's subsequent shift to the southern theater of war, which lasted until Yorktown in 1781, can be traced to the recognition that the northern Continental Army had become a formidable obstacle. The 1778 alliance with France, sealed by Washington's demonstrated military competence, proved decisive: by Yorktown, French and American forces operated as true partners, a coordination impossible without the confidence 1778 had built. Washington's 1778 thus connects directly to the war's outcome — American victory and the founding of the republic.
