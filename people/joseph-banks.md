# Joseph Banks (1743–1820)

**Category**: Key Figures

**Key Dates**: Born February 13, 1743 (Lincolnshire, England); died June 19, 1820 (Isleworth, London)

---

## Summary

Sir Joseph Banks was a British naturalist, explorer, and botanist who transformed 1778 into a turning point in the professionalization and patronage of science. On November 30, 1778, he was elected President of the Royal Society—a position he would hold for 41 years, making him the longest-serving president in its history. Banks brought to the presidency an unprecedented wealth of scientific experience: he had sailed with Captain James Cook on the HMS Endeavour's first voyage (1768–1771), collecting approximately 30,000 plant specimens and documenting nearly 1,400 plant species previously unknown to Europeans. By 1778, he had become the indispensable scientific advisor to King George III and the royal government, with decisive influence over the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew, British exploration policy, and the scientific infrastructure of the British Empire. His election marked the ascendancy of empirical naturalism and overseas scientific patronage as tools of state power—a transformation perfectly timed with the Franco-American alliance and the global expansion of the Revolutionary War that same year.

---

## Significance

Banks's 1778 elevation to the Royal Society presidency represents three convergent transitions in 18th-century science:

**1. The Professionalization of Natural History**  
Banks's presidency marked the rise of the working naturalist-patron—a figure who combined field exploration (Cook's voyage), specimen collection, taxonomic expertise, and institutional power. Where the Society had previously been a forum of gentleman amateurs, Banks anchored it in systematic empiricism and global exploration. His decision to hold the presidency for four decades ensured that the Society would become a coordinating center for British science worldwide.

**2. Science as Imperial Instrument**  
Banks advised King George III on colonial policy, particularly regarding Australia and the Pacific. He championed British settlement in Australia and coordinated with the government for over twenty years, making science inseparable from territorial expansion and resource extraction. The same year he became Royal Society president, Cook was completing his second voyage in the Pacific (returning March 1779), and Banks's influence over what Britain would do with Cook's discoveries—and where the next explorers would go—was absolute.

**3. The Botanical Garden as Knowledge Infrastructure**  
Banks transformed the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew from a royal curiosity into the world's leading botanical institution, dispatching collectors to every corner of the globe and organizing systematic study of the specimens they brought home. By 1778, Kew had become the technological heart of British horticultural advantage—the place where tropical plants were acclimatized, bred, and studied before being deployed in colonial agriculture or disseminated to allied nations. Kew's scientific ascendancy ran parallel to Britain's naval dominance and the global reach of its trade and empire.

Banks's 1778 presidency therefore marks the moment when the Enlightenment's empirical naturalism became an instrument of state power, and when the isolated explorer (Cook in the Pacific) became integrated into an institutional network (the Royal Society, Kew, the Admiralty, the Crown) that could magnify discovery into geopolitical advantage.

## Life and Career

**Early Years and Enthusiasm for Botany**  
Banks was born on February 13, 1743, in Lincolnshire to a wealthy landed family. His father, William Banks, owned extensive estates. At age 21, Joseph inherited Revesby Abbey—one of England's great estates—and an annual income of approximately £6,000, making him one of England's wealthiest men and freeing him to pursue science as a gentleman-patron. He credited his first interest in botany to the wildflowers near Eton, where he was educated.

**The Cook Voyage (1768–1771)**  
In 1768, at age 25, Banks joined Captain James Cook's first expedition aboard the HMS Endeavour as a naturalist and gentleman scientist. He brought with him a small team of specialists: Daniel Solander and Herman Spöring (fellow naturalists), the artists Alexander Buchan and Sydney Parkinson, and several servants. Over three years, the expedition visited Brazil, Tahiti, the New Zealand coast, and the eastern coast of Australia—territories unmapped by European science. Banks's team accumulated approximately 30,000 plant specimens and documented roughly 1,400 plant species previously unknown to Europeans. The return voyage made Banks an international scientific celebrity and established him as the era's preeminent collector of exotic natural knowledge.

**Preeminence in British Science (1770s)**  
After his return, Banks became an invaluable advisor to the Crown and the intellectual hub of London's scientific circles. He advised King George III on botanical and agricultural matters, had decisive influence over the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew, and cultivated relations with scientists across Europe and America despite the political turbulence of the Revolutionary War era. His wealth and collection made him a magnet for naturalists, artists, and explorers seeking patronage or knowledge.

**Banks in 1778: A Pivotal Year**  
Beyond his election to the Royal Society presidency on November 30, 1778, the year marked a convergence of Banks's scientific and political influence. Captain Cook's third Pacific voyage (1776–1779) was underway, and Banks—though he had declined to sail on the third voyage himself due to a dispute with the Admiralty over cabin arrangements—maintained active contact with Cook's naturalists and monitored the expedition's progress. Cook would be killed in Hawaii on February 14, 1779, but in 1778 he was still alive and exploring the Pacific Northwest, and Banks was positioning himself to process the expedition's botanical and zoological collections upon their return.

In 1778, Banks also deepened his relationship with the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew, working with director William Aiton to expand the gardens' collection of specimens from collectors worldwide. Francis Masson, the first official plant collector dispatched from Kew (at Banks's instigation), had completed his landmark South Africa expedition (1772–1774) and was preparing further travels; Banks coordinated his assignment to Madeira and the Canary Islands. The year also saw Banks maintaining remarkable scientific correspondence across the front lines of war: he continued exchanging letters with French naturalists despite the Anglo-French war that Britain declared in March 1778, and corresponded with American scientists including Philadelphia's American Philosophical Society, of which he was a foreign member. His ability to maintain transatlantic scientific networks despite geopolitical rupture demonstrated science's Enlightenment-era claim to transcend national conflict.

The total solar eclipse of June 24, 1778—recorded in North America and seen as a partial eclipse across Europe—engaged the kind of coordinated observational network the Royal Society had long fostered. Such collective astronomical and meteorological projects, which defined Enlightenment scientific practice, were exactly the enterprises Banks would systematize after taking the presidency later that year.

**Royal Society Presidency (1778–1820)**  
On November 30, 1778, Banks succeeded Sir John Pringle as President of the Royal Society at age 35. He held the position for 41 years until his death in 1820, making him the longest-serving president in the Society's history. During his tenure, he:
- Fostered international scientific cooperation even across wartime divisions (corresponding with American and French naturalists despite Revolutionary and Napoleonic conflicts).
- Made the Royal Society the coordinating center for British exploration and scientific patronage.
- Supported major expeditions by George Vancouver and William Bligh.
- Backed geologist William Smith's pioneering geological mapping project.
- Ensured that Kew Gardens became the world's leading botanical institution.

**Collections and Correspondence Network**  
Banks's herbarium at 32 Soho Square, London—his private residence and scientific headquarters—was by 1778 one of the world's largest collections of dried plant specimens, with over 30,000 sheets from the Cook voyage alone, supplemented by acquisitions from European and colonial sources. It functioned as an unofficial national botanical archive, open to visiting naturalists and correspondents. Banks employed Daniel Solander as librarian and curator, managing a circulation of specimens, drawings, and letters that networked Banks to naturalists across Europe and the Americas. His library, accumulated over decades, contained thousands of volumes and thousands of original botanical illustrations by Sydney Parkinson and others from the Endeavour voyage.

By 1778, Banks's correspondence list included figures such as Carolus Linnaeus the Younger (son of the recently deceased Carl Linnaeus), French botanist Antoine Laurent de Jussieu, Swedish naturalist Daniel Solander, and American scientists in the American Philosophical Society. The war strained but did not sever these connections: Banks and Franklin, both members of the Royal Society and the American Philosophical Society, continued to regard each other as colleagues even as their governments fought.

**Later Years and Legacy**  
Banks remained active in science and colonial policy until his death on June 19, 1820, at age 77. He was created a baronet in 1781, a recognition of his influence. His legacy includes the introduction of hundreds of plant species to European cultivation, the professionalization of botanical science through institutional networks, and the integration of natural history into the infrastructure of imperial expansion.
