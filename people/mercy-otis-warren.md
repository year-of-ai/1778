# Mercy Otis Warren (1728–1814)

**Category**: Key Figures  
**Key figures**: Mercy Otis Warren, James Otis Jr., Hannah Winthrop, James Warren

## Summary

Mercy Otis Warren was the most prominent female political voice of 1778, a year in which she published anonymous political poetry, maintained an active political correspondence network, and began gathering materials for what would become the first major historical account of the American Revolution written by a woman. In October 1778, the Boston Gazette published her poem "The Genius of America Weeping the Absurd Follies of the Day," a rare contemporary critique of wartime profiteering and the perceived decline of public virtue under the strain of conflict. The poem, published anonymously to protect her reputation (as was customary for female political writers), demonstrated that women participated in public debate through disguised authorship and that female political commentary existed in the revolutionary press.

Warren's 1778 correspondence was extensive and politically sophisticated. Her documented letters to Hannah Winthrop in January, February, August, and December 1778 reveal a woman intimately engaged with Revolutionary politics, battlefield conditions, and the moral character of the Republic under siege. She observed and lamented the death of her brother James Otis Jr. in December 1778—the former political radical and leading voice of colonial resistance to the Stamp Act—and her grief mingled with political reflection on the Revolution her brother had helped to inspire. She also documented her husband James Warren's electoral defeat in May 1778 when he lost reelection to the Massachusetts House of Representatives, an event that combined personal disappointment with political significance in her family and correspondence.

By 1778, Warren had already established herself as a playwright and political satirist—her plays attacking British policies had circulated (often anonymously or pseudonymously) since the 1770s. Her 1778 activities mark a turning point in her career: while still writing political poetry and correspondence, she began systematically gathering materials and correspondence for a comprehensive history of the American Revolution. This archival work, conducted over the subsequent decades, would culminate in her *History of the Rise, Progress, and Termination of the American Revolution*, published in 1805. Her 1778 letters and documents became part of this historical archive, making her both a witness to 1778 events and a historian consciously recording them for posterity.

Warren's 1778 experience illustrates how educated women of the Revolutionary elite participated in political culture through writing—correspondence, anonymous publication, and historical documentation—while being excluded from formal political office and military service. Her position as the wife of a politically active man and sister to a famous radical gave her access to political information and networks, which she transformed into her own political voice through written expression.

## Anonymous Political Poetry and the Boston Gazette

Warren's October 1778 poem "The Genius of America Weeping the Absurd Follies of the Day," published anonymously in the *Boston Gazette*, exemplifies the indirect channel through which educated women entered public debate during the Revolutionary era. The poem attacked wartime profiteering and luxury consumption — specifically the tendency of merchant families to enrich themselves through privateering and speculation while soldiers endured the hardships of [Valley Forge](../military/valley-forge-encampment.md). Its critique engaged directly with the [revolutionary war financing](../economics/revolutionary-war-financing-1778.md) crisis: Warren argued that private profit-seeking at public expense corrupted the republican virtue the Revolution claimed to serve. The *Boston Gazette*, founded in 1719 and one of New England's most widely read Patriot newspapers, gave her poem significant circulation — though readers would not have known a woman wrote it.

This poem continued Warren's established strategy of anonymous political authorship. Her earlier plays *The Adulateur* (1772) and *The Group* (1775) had circulated anonymously, attacking Governor Thomas Hutchinson and the Loyalist elite in satirical dramatic form. By 1778, anonymous publication was not a compromise for Warren but a considered political strategy: it allowed her to shape public opinion while navigating the social convention that barred women from explicitly claiming a public political voice.

## The Correspondence Network of 1778

Warren's political correspondence in 1778 constituted a sophisticated information network. Her documented letters to Hannah Winthrop — wife of Harvard mathematics professor John Winthrop — in January, February, August, and December 1778 combined personal news with systematic political and military analysis. She documented Massachusetts public response to the [Carlisle Peace Commission](../politics/carlisle-peace-commission.md)'s peace overtures, reporting in summer 1778 that Patriot opinion firmly rejected British concessions as too little and too late. She debated with correspondents the governance questions posed by the [Articles of Confederation](../politics/articles-of-confederation-1778.md), analyzing whether a confederation strong enough to wage war could simultaneously preserve the liberties it claimed to defend.

Warren also corresponded directly with John Adams — then in Paris negotiating the [Franco-American Alliance](../politics/franco-american-alliance.md) — and later with Thomas Jefferson, establishing herself as a woman whose political analysis leading Revolutionary figures actively sought. Her parallel friendship with [Abigail Adams](../people/abigail-adams.md) created a small but significant women's political commentary network in Massachusetts: both women were educationally privileged, politically informed, managing households during their husbands' absence, and determined to record the Revolution as they experienced it.

## James Otis Jr. and Family Loss in 1778

Mercy Otis Warren's brother James Otis Jr. (1725–1783) — the Boston lawyer whose fiery speeches against writs of assistance in the 1760s had helped inspire colonial resistance — was alive in 1778 but largely incapacitated. Since September 5, 1769, when British customs commissioner John Robinson struck him over the head in a confrontation at the British Coffee House in Boston, Otis had suffered progressive episodes of mental confusion and instability that removed him from public life. By 1778, he lived under family care, occasionally lucid but incapable of sustained political activity. Warren's 1778 correspondence reveals grief over her brother's condition alongside political reflection on the Revolution he had helped to inspire but could not now celebrate. Otis died on May 23, 1783, struck by lightning at a house in Andover, Massachusetts — long after the decisive events he had set in motion had transformed the political world he once dominated.

His condition cast a shadow over Warren's 1778 reflections on the Revolution's personal costs: the man whose arguments against parliamentary taxation had galvanized colonial resistance could not participate in or even comprehend the alliance with France that made independence possible.

## Beginning the Historical Record

The systematic character of Warren's 1778 correspondence reflects a conscious archival decision. Her letters from this period forward show increasing attention to documentation — she preserved incoming correspondence, retained copies of outgoing letters, and included factual precision about dates, events, and personalities that would matter only to a future historian. She was not merely exchanging letters but building a source archive for a comprehensive history of the Revolution she intended to write.

Her *History of the Rise, Progress, and Termination of the American Revolution* (3 vols., 1805) drew directly on this correspondence archive, including her 1778 letters and observations. Warren's decision to begin consciously recording the Revolution's history in 1778 — the year of the decisive French alliance — was not coincidental: she recognized that this turning point required documented explanation for future generations. She and Abigail Adams shared this historical consciousness: both women's 1778 correspondence reads, in retrospect, as the work of people who understood they were living through events that posterity would study.

## Significance

Mercy Otis Warren's 1778 activities demonstrate that women's political agency during the Revolutionary War operated through the written word when formal political institutions were closed to them. Her anonymous publication in the Boston Gazette shows that female political commentary existed in the Revolutionary press, though attributed to male pseudonyms or left unsigned to protect reputation. Her decision in 1778 to begin systematic historical documentation foreshadowed her landmark contribution to Revolutionary historiography—she became the only prominent female participant in the War to publish a comprehensive history. Her 1778 correspondence, later preserved and published, provided historians with eyewitness accounts of home-front conditions, family experiences, and the political culture of Massachusetts. Warren's career demonstrates that women of the Revolutionary generation did not merely experience the War passively but actively shaped its narrative and meaning through their writing and documentation.
