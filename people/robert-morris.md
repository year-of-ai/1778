# Robert Morris (1734–1806)

**Category**: Key Figures  
**Key figures**: Robert Morris Jr. (1734–1806), John Holker (1745–1822), Thomas Willing (1731–1821)

## Early Life and Career

Robert Morris Jr. was born on January 20, 1734, in Liverpool, England, the son of a tobacco factor who had emigrated earlier to the Chesapeake region. At approximately thirteen years of age — around 1747 — Morris crossed the Atlantic to join his father at Oxford, Maryland. After his father's accidental death in 1750 (shot by the wad of a cannon fired in his honor), the sixteen-year-old Morris was placed under the guardianship of a Philadelphia merchant and quickly apprenticed into the counting house of Thomas and Charles Willing. His aptitude for commerce was immediately apparent, and by 1754 — at age twenty — he was admitted as a junior partner in what became Willing, Morris & Company.

The firm became one of the dominant commercial houses in colonial America, trading in tobacco, flour, and dry goods across the Atlantic world. Morris developed close ties with correspondents in London, Amsterdam, and Paris, and built a personal credit network that would prove indispensable during the Revolutionary War. By the early 1770s he was reputedly the wealthiest merchant in America, with a fortune estimated at several hundred thousand pounds sterling.

## Congressional Finance (1775–1778)

Morris was elected to the Continental Congress in 1775 and became one of its most industrious finance and procurement officers. Despite initially opposing independence on practical commercial grounds — he believed premature separation would be economically disastrous — he nonetheless signed the Declaration of Independence in August 1776 alongside his congressional colleagues.

His most consequential wartime contribution came through his leadership of the **Secret Committee of Correspondence** and later the **Committee on Commerce** (sometimes called the Secret Committee of Trade), which procured munitions, powder, cloth, and other war materiel from France, the Netherlands, and the Caribbean. Between 1775 and 1778, these committees arranged millions of livres' worth of arms shipments from France through intermediaries including Caron de Beaumarchais's front company *Rodrigue Hortalez et Compagnie*, which shipped approximately 200 cannons, 25,000 muskets, and large quantities of powder and clothing to the Continental Army before France's formal entry into the war.

As chairman of the Finance Committee, Morris performed what amounted to the functions of a Treasury in a government that possessed none: advancing personal funds to pay troops, procuring credit from foreign merchants, and arranging exchanges of American tobacco and grain for French munitions. By his own later accounting he advanced over one million dollars from personal funds during this period, though the precise boundary between personal profit and public service remained contested by political opponents.

### The Deane Affair

Morris's congressional tenure was shadowed by the controversy surrounding Silas Deane, the American agent in Paris whose procurement contracts overlapped with Morris's committee work. In 1778, Deane was recalled from Paris amid accusations that he had mingled public and private accounts. Critics, led by Arthur Lee, alleged that Morris had similarly profited improperly from war contracts steered to Willing, Morris & Company. Morris vigorously denied the allegations and commissioned a public accounting of his transactions, but the affair generated lasting enmity from political rivals and contributed to the atmosphere of suspicion surrounding wartime finance (see [Revolutionary War Financing in 1778](../economics/revolutionary-war-financing-1778.md)).

## The Transition Year: 1778

### Leave from Congress and Public Controversy

In early 1778, Morris obtained leave from Congress to address the accusations arising from the Deane controversy. He spent several months preparing a formal public defense of his financial accounts. The defense was largely accepted by his congressional supporters, though his political opponents — particularly the Lee-Adams faction — continued to question the propriety of war contracts awarded to firms in which Morris held an interest.

### Resignation and State Legislature

In July 1778, Morris formally resigned his seat in the Continental Congress to accept election to the Pennsylvania General Assembly, where he joined a conservative, constitutionalist faction advocating reform of Pennsylvania's radical 1776 constitution. The Pennsylvania Constitution of 1776 had created a unicameral legislature without an executive — a structure Morris and his mercantile allies considered dangerously populist. His legislative work in 1778–1779 reflected the broader social conflict between merchants and artisan-class radicals that was reshaping Pennsylvania politics in parallel with the war.

### Agent for French Consul General John Holker

Simultaneously with his Pennsylvania legislative duties, Morris secured appointment in mid-1778 as commercial agent for **John Holker** (1745–1822), the French consul general in Philadelphia. Holker, himself a textile manufacturer from Lancashire who had settled in France and served the French crown, was tasked with coordinating French military provisioning in the four Mid-Atlantic states: Pennsylvania, Delaware, New York, and New Jersey.

Morris held this appointment from mid-1778 through January 1780, during which time he arranged the supply of beef, flour, and naval stores to French forces operating in North America — including the squadron of the Comte d'Estaing (see [Comte d'Estaing](comte-destaing.md)) and subsequently the French army under Rochambeau. The Holker contract was enormously lucrative, generating commissions and profits that substantially augmented Morris's personal fortune at a moment when Continental currency was collapsing (the Continental dollar lost over 95% of its face value between 1777 and 1781 — see [Continental Dollar Crisis of 1778](../economics/continental-dollar-crisis.md)).

Morris's access to hard currency through the Holker arrangement — French gold and silver to pay for American provisions — gave him a capital advantage over competitors tied to depreciating Continental notes, allowing him to acquire land, goods, and debt instruments at distressed prices. Critics argued this constituted insider profiteering; Morris maintained it was fair commercial compensation for managing complex, high-risk supply chains.

## Later Career and Legacy

The 1778 transition positioned Morris for his most historically significant role: when Congress, facing total financial collapse in 1781, appointed him Superintendent of Finance (February 1781), he accepted on the condition of sweeping authority. He established the **Bank of North America** in January 1782 — the first chartered commercial bank in the United States — and used a combination of foreign loans, personal credit, and tight fiscal discipline to stabilize Continental finances sufficiently to fund the Yorktown campaign of 1781 and sustain the army through the final stages of the war.

The commercial networks, Franco-American supply relationships, and credit instruments Morris had built between 1775 and 1780 — including the Holker arrangement — provided the institutional foundation for his Superintendent role. His experience as one of the wealthiest merchants in America, combined with his practical knowledge of French government contracting, made him uniquely positioned to bridge the gap between America's chronically underfunded government and its international creditors.

Morris ended his life in financial ruin: postwar land speculation in western territories and the subsequent financial panic of 1797 erased his fortune and landed him in debtors' prison from 1798 to 1801. He died on May 8, 1806, in Philadelphia — impoverished but still celebrated by contemporaries as the man who had "financed the Revolution." His career encapsulates the paradoxes of early American capitalism: the same willingness to take financial risk that made Morris indispensable to the revolutionary cause ultimately destroyed him.

## Significance

Morris's 1778 transition from congressional finance to private commerce and Franco-American supply contracting proved historically consequential. His merchant expertise and existing trade networks with France — built during his Finance Committee years — made him invaluable as an intermediary between French military suppliers and American forces. His work as agent for John Holker established the commercial infrastructure that sustained French military operations in North America at a critical moment when American finances were collapsing and reliance on foreign aid was becoming absolute.

Morris's activities in 1778 foreshadowed his later role as Superintendent of Finance (1781–1784), when he would emerge as the primary architect of American Revolutionary finance, stabilizing the economy through the Bank of North America and domestic and foreign credit instruments. His career exemplifies the entanglement of private commerce and public finance in the Revolution, and his 1778 position as a Franco-American trade intermediary proved essential to the alliance's material success.
