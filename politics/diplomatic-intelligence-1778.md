# Diplomatic Intelligence & Espionage Networks of 1778

**Category**: Political Events  
**Key figures**: Benjamin Franklin, Silas Deane, Arthur Lee, Edward Bancroft, Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais, Comte de Vergennes, John Paul Jones, Lord Stormont

## Summary

The year 1778 witnessed the consolidation of American intelligence operations in France under Benjamin Franklin's direction, centered on securing and implementing the [Treaty of Alliance](franco-american-alliance.md) signed on February 6. Franklin, who had arrived in France in December 1776, orchestrated a sophisticated network of diplomatic channels, secret correspondence, counter-surveillance, propaganda, and clandestine military operations from his residence at the **Hôtel de Valentinois** in Passy. Working alongside fellow diplomat Silas Deane and secretly supported by French playwright Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais, Franklin coordinated with Comte de Vergennes to finalize formal military and economic commitments to American independence.

This diplomatic triumph was shadowed by penetration from the outset. Franklin's own private secretary, **Edward Bancroft** (1745–1821), was operating simultaneously as a paid British intelligence agent throughout 1778, depositing American diplomatic secrets in a hollow tree in the Tuileries Gardens each Tuesday evening at half past nine for collection by British contacts. Bancroft received an annual retainer from British intelligence — reportedly beginning at £500 and later raised to as much as £1,000 — together with the promise of a lifetime pension, and his reports reached London within days of their composition in Passy. The intelligence apparatus also extended beyond formal diplomacy to encompass privateering coordination with naval commanders like John Paul Jones, cipher communications, propaganda dissemination through Franklin's own printing press, and counter-intelligence measures against British surveillance.

## The Passy Mission: Operations and Structure

Franklin's residence at the Hôtel de Valentinois, provided rent-free by wealthy merchant Jacques Donatien Le Ray de Chaumont, served as the nerve center of American intelligence activities in Europe. Franklin operated there as the senior member of a three-man commission alongside Silas Deane and Arthur Lee, though internal tensions among the commissioners frequently complicated intelligence security.

On **September 14, 1778**, Congress formally appointed Franklin **Minister Plenipotentiary** to France — the first American to hold that rank with any foreign government — consolidating diplomatic authority in a single figure and rationalizing the Passy mission's command structure. This appointment came after months of dysfunctional three-way commission governance in which the commissioners often disagreed about basic operational matters.

Franklin maintained a **small printing press** at Passy, producing French-language pamphlets, diplomatic correspondence, and propaganda. The press served an intelligence function: Franklin could produce and distribute materials shaping French and European public opinion without routing content through official channels susceptible to interception. He used the press to publish forged documents as well — including a fabricated "Supplement to the Boston Independent Chronicle" purporting to document British-sponsored atrocities against American civilians, designed to harden French and British public opinion against continued war.

## Edward Bancroft: The Spy Inside the Mission

### Recruitment and Access

Edward Bancroft was an American-born naturalist, physician, and novelist who had established himself in London before the Revolution. He had known Benjamin Franklin since at least the 1760s through shared scientific interests — Bancroft published work on natural history and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society — and this prior relationship made him a natural and trusted choice when Franklin needed a private secretary in Paris. What Franklin did not know was that Bancroft had been recruited by British intelligence before he joined the Passy mission, reportedly cultivated through Paul Wentworth, a Loyalist with ties to the New Hampshire Wentworth family who served as a British intelligence officer in Paris.

Bancroft attended the negotiations leading to the [Treaty of Alliance](franco-american-alliance.md), had access to the commissioners' private correspondence, and observed diplomatic conversations at first hand. His position gave British intelligence a contemporaneous window into the mission's most sensitive activities.

### Tradecraft and Communication

Bancroft's operational security method was systematic and effective. He wrote his intelligence reports in **invisible ink** between the lines of what appeared to be ordinary letters on the topic of the gallnut trade. Each Tuesday evening at **half past nine**, he deposited a sealed bottle containing his reports in the hollow of a box tree on the south terrace of the **Tuileries Gardens**. A British contact — operating under the cover of a casual stroller — would collect the bottle within thirty minutes and dispatch the contents to London, early in 1778 via Lord Stormont, the British ambassador in Paris, and after Stormont's recall that March through other handlers and the diplomatic pouch to Whitehall.

Bancroft also conducted personal meetings with Wentworth in Paris and, on at least one occasion in January 1778, traveled to London for direct consultations with British officials — a visit he explained to Franklin as a private business trip. These meetings allowed British intelligence to task Bancroft with specific collection requirements in real time. In January 1778, for example, Bancroft briefed British ministers on the state of alliance negotiations at a critical juncture — intelligence that directly informed British decisions about how to respond before the Treaty of Alliance was formally signed on February 6.

### Detection and Legacy

During the war, neither Franklin nor American counter-intelligence detected Bancroft's treachery. Arthur Lee, the most suspicious of the three commissioners, frequently raised alarms about security — he accused Silas Deane of financial impropriety and suspected Bancroft of irregularities — but Lee's general paranoia diluted the credibility of his specific warnings. Bancroft remained in place throughout the war, continuing to supply intelligence until the cessation of hostilities in 1783.

The full extent of Bancroft's espionage was not confirmed until 1889, when British Foreign Office archives were opened and his reports were identified. The revelation — more than a century after the fact — demonstrated both the effectiveness of British intelligence tradecraft in the 1778 era and the profound vulnerability of the American diplomatic mission. Bancroft's case became a foundational study in intelligence history: a hostile agent inserted at the highest level of an adversary's mission, undetected for the entire duration of an operation.

## The Beaumarchais-Hortalez Network as Intelligence Infrastructure

Before the formal alliance, the covert supply operation organized by Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais through the fictional firm **Rodrigue Hortalez et Cie** functioned simultaneously as a logistics operation and a cover for intelligence activity. Beaumarchais, already famous as the author of *The Barber of Seville* (first performed in 1775), established Hortalez in 1776 with a 1-million-livre subsidy from the French crown and additional Spanish funds. The firm's ostensible commercial activities — importing tobacco and other goods — provided cover for shipping gunpowder, cannon, muskets, and clothing to the Continental Army; Hortalez supplied approximately **90 percent of the gunpowder** used by the Continental Army in 1776–1777.

In 1778, with open alliance replacing covert support, Hortalez's role shifted toward coordinating specific military supply requirements identified through intelligence. Beaumarchais remained in Franklin's orbit as an informal intelligence contact who maintained relationships with French court officials not directly accessible through diplomatic channels. His theatrical social network — extensive, cross-class, and difficult for British surveillance to monitor — provided a supplementary communication pathway for sensitive information. The [Franco-American Alliance Nexus](franco-american-alliance-nexus.md) synthesis traces how this clandestine supply infrastructure transitioned into the formal military partnership of 1778.

## Privateering Operations: Intelligence Fusion with Naval Warfare

### Coordination with John Paul Jones

Franklin's intelligence role extended to coordinating privateering and naval raiding operations designed to stretch British resources and demonstrate American offensive reach. **John Paul Jones** visited Passy and corresponded frequently with Franklin throughout 1778, treating the American mission as his operational sponsor. Franklin secured Jones's commission, advocated for him with the French Navy Ministry, and coordinated his raids against British coastal targets.

Jones's most dramatic 1778 operations unfolded in April. On **April 22–23, 1778**, commanding the sloop *Ranger*, Jones raided the British port of **Whitehaven** on the Cumberland coast — the first hostile landing on British soil since the Anglo-Dutch Wars of the seventeenth century. He spiked the harbor guns and set fire to a coal vessel before withdrawing. On **April 23**, he raided **St. Mary's Isle** off the Scottish coast, attempting to seize the Earl of Selkirk as a hostage for prisoner exchange (the Earl was absent, so the mission failed). On **April 24**, Jones engaged and captured the Royal Navy brig **HMS Drake** in the North Channel — the first capture of a Royal Navy warship by an American naval vessel in British home waters.

These operations were not militarily decisive in material terms, but their psychological and propaganda impact was considerable. News that American raiders had landed on British soil and defeated a Royal Navy vessel in its own home waters spread through European press and demonstrated that the French alliance had emboldened American operations. Franklin used reports of the raids in his propaganda operations, and they strengthened Vergennes's confidence that the alliance was producing the strategic pressure on Britain that France required.

### Intelligence Value of Privateering

Beyond direct naval action, privateering served an intelligence-collection function. Captured British vessels yielded crew members who could be interrogated about naval dispositions, convoy schedules, and British operational plans. Letters of marque coordinated through the Passy mission created a network of armed vessels crossing the Atlantic whose captains could be tasked with observing British fleet movements. See [American Revolutionary War Financing in 1778](../economics/revolutionary-war-financing-1778.md) for the economic dimension of privateering as a complement to French loans.

## Cipher Communications and Secret Correspondence

The American commissioners used multiple **cipher systems** for communication with the Continental Congress, rotating them periodically as security concerns arose. Franklin employed nomenclators (code books in which words, names, and phrases were replaced by numbers or letter groups) as well as letter-substitution ciphers in dispatches routed through multiple European cities to reduce the risk of interception on the Atlantic crossing.

The vulnerability introduced by Bancroft, however, meant that any dispatch prepared in Passy before it left the building was potentially compromised. British intelligence thus sometimes read congressional directives to the commissioners before the commissioners had opportunity to act on them — a penetration so deep that Britain could calibrate its diplomatic counter-moves in near-real time. Britain's Secretary of State for the Southern Department, Lord Weymouth, and his successors received summaries of American diplomatic correspondence throughout 1778, a fact that explains several otherwise puzzling instances of British diplomatic prescience during the period of alliance negotiations.

Franklin also used his **Passy printing press** to produce diplomatic materials in formats designed to prevent forgery and authentication: controlled-distribution documents bearing distinctive paper stock or typographical marks legible only to their intended recipients — a primitive but functional authentication system for the pre-industrial era.

## Counter-Intelligence and Internal Security

### The Arthur Lee Problem

The three-commissioner structure created endemic counter-intelligence difficulties. Arthur Lee, the third commissioner, was deeply suspicious of both Deane and Bancroft and wrote repeatedly to Congressional allies alleging financial misconduct, security breaches, and loyalist sympathies within the mission. Lee was correct in suspecting Bancroft was a security risk, but his inability to produce proof — and his general tendency to level accusations broadly — made his warnings dismissible.

Lee's disputes with Deane were so severe that they reached Congress and contributed to Deane's recall in 1778. The Deane-Lee controversy effectively paralyzed the commission's internal deliberations for months and was exploited by British intelligence to introduce additional confusion. Bancroft himself quietly encouraged congressional suspicion of Deane, knowing that Deane's recall would reduce the likelihood that any post-departure audit would expose Bancroft's own activities.

### Franklin's Approach to Security

Franklin's personal approach to security was more fatalistic than systematic. His famous reported remark — that three people could keep a secret only if two of them were dead — reflected a recognition that the Passy mission's security was inherently limited. He concentrated on ensuring that the most critical diplomatic maneuvers were conducted face-to-face with Vergennes at Versailles, where British surveillance was more difficult, rather than in written correspondence. Key commitments related to the Treaty of Alliance, the dispatch of the [Comte d'Estaing's](../people/comte-destaing.md) naval squadron, and French loan terms were communicated verbally and only later committed to writing.

Franklin also cultivated relationships with French counter-intelligence officials, who were aware of British surveillance operations in Paris and occasionally fed deliberately misleading information to British agents to protect the alliance's most sensitive operational planning. This cooperation with Vergennes's intelligence apparatus extended American operational security beyond what the Passy mission could have maintained independently.

## Strategic Impact and Legacy

### Achieving the Primary Objective

Franklin's 1778 intelligence network achieved the central strategic objective of the Revolutionary War: transforming an internal colonial rebellion into a global conflict by securing French military alliance and economic support. This diplomatic-intelligence fusion was essential to American survival; without French credit secured through Franklin's operations, the Continental Army could not have sustained the war effort. For the army's direct benefit from this transformed strategic situation, see [Valley Forge Encampment](../military/valley-forge-encampment.md) — the crisis the 1778 alliance directly alleviated — and [Battle of Monmouth](../military/battle-of-monmouth.md), the first major engagement after France's entry changed the war's strategic character.

The rejection of Britain's simultaneous [Carlisle Peace Commission](carlisle-peace-commission.md) in 1778 — Congress refused any offer short of independence — was itself partly an intelligence-informed decision: Franklin's Passy network provided Congress with early information about the Commission's terms and demonstrated that the Commission represented British weakness rather than genuine conciliation.

### Intelligence Doctrine Precedent

The intelligence operations Franklin built in 1778 established precedent: his counter-surveillance methods, use of cipher communications, coordination of privateers as intelligence-collection assets, and manipulation of propaganda became templates for early American intelligence doctrine. The Committee of Secret Correspondence (renamed the Committee of Foreign Affairs in 1777), which nominally oversaw diplomatic intelligence, received Franklin's reports and managed the cipher systems — creating an institutional framework, however rudimentary, for centralized intelligence coordination.

The presence of British agent Bancroft within the delegation — undetected during the war, revealed definitively in 1889 — demonstrated the vulnerability of diplomatic missions to espionage and the difficulty of compartmentalizing secrets in the pre-industrial era. The episode foreshadowed persistent tensions between diplomatic openness and security that would define intelligence operations into the modern period.
